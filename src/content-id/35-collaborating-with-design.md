## **The Mockup That Made Our Designer Quit (Almost)**

Early in my career, I handed a designer a rough wireframe I'd sketched myself, complete with specific button placements, exact copy, and a particular color scheme I'd personally picked out. "Here's what I need," I said. "Just make it look polished."

She looked at the wireframe, then looked at me, and said something I'll never forget: "So you've already designed this. What exactly do you need me for?"

I didn't have a good answer. I'd treated design as a visual polish layer applied _after_ the real thinking (which I'd apparently already done myself) was complete—essentially asking a trained UX professional to be my personal Photoshop filter. She stayed at the company, thankfully, but our working relationship took months to genuinely recover, and I Jangan blame her one bit for the initial frustration.

This is one of the most common and damaging mistakes PMs make: **treating design as decoration rather than as a discipline with its own genuine expertise, methodology, and critical thinking.** Design isn't about making things pretty. It's about deeply understanding user behavior, cognitive load, interaction patterns, and translating business and user needs into an experience that actually works—which requires exactly the same kind of rigorous, expert thinking you'd (hopefully) never dream of Kedua,-guessing from your tim engineering on a technical architecture decision.

Let's fix this relationship.

## **PM vs Designer: Who Actually Owns UX?**

Here's a genuinely nuanced question that different companies and teams answer differently, and there's no single universally correct answer—but there is a genuinely unhealthy answer to avoid.

**The unhealthy version:** PM dictates a specific solution (like my wireframe disaster above), and design is relegated to visual execution of an already-determined interaction pattern. This wastes design's actual expertise and typically produces worse outcomes than genuine collaborative problem-solving would.

**The healthier version:** PM owns defining the _problem_ clearly (what user need are we solving, what business constraints exist, what does success look like), while design owns exploring and determining the best _solution_ to that problem, with genuine collaborative dialogue throughout—not a rigid handoff in either direction.

**A useful mental model:** Think of it as PM owning the "what and why," design owning the "how" (specifically the experience/interaction "how," while engineering owns the technical "how"), with genuine overlap and dialogue at the boundaries rather than rigid, siloed lanes.

## **Design Thinking Integration: Beyond the Buzzword**

"Design Thinking" has become such an overused corporate buzzword that it's easy to forget the genuinely useful methodology underneath the jargon.

**The core phases (IDEO's classic framework):**

**Empathize:** Deeply understand user needs through research (this overlaps significantly with the Discovery work covered in Chapter 4—ideally, PM and design collaborate on this phase together, not in separate silos).

**Define:** Synthesize research into a clear, specific problem statement. This is genuinely collaborative PM/design territory—both disciplines should align on what problem is actually being solved before jumping to solutions.

**Ideate:** Generate a wide range of potential solutions before converging on one. This is where design expertise genuinely shines, and where PMs should resist the urge to anchor the team on their own predetermined solution too early, potentially foreclosing better options that haven't been explored yet.

**Prototype:** Build low-fidelity representations of potential solutions quickly, to enable fast testing without the sunk cost of full production-quality builds.

**Test:** Get the prototype in front of real users, gathering feedback before committing engineering resources to full build-out.

**Why PMs should genuinely internalize this, not just delegate it to design:** Understanding this process helps you recognize when you're inadvertently skipping steps (jumping straight from a vague problem statement to a specific solution without genuine ideation or testing), and helps you advocate for appropriate time/resources for design to do this work properly, rather than treating it as an unnecessary delay before "real" development work begins.

## **Usability Testing: Whose Job Is It, Really?**

**Common dysfunction:** Usability testing gets treated as purely design's responsibility, with PM only reviewing a summary report afterward, missing valuable firsthand context and nuance that comes from actually observing user struggles directly.

**A healthier approach:** PM and design ideally observe usability sessions together (even if design leads the actual moderation), because watching a real user struggle with something you both believed was intuitive is a uniquely powerful, humbling, and clarifying experience that a secondhand summary report simply cannot replicate.

**What to actually watch for during usability testing:**

- Where users hesitate or express confusion (even if they eventually succeed)
- Where users' mental models Jangan match how the system actually works
- What users say aloud versus what they actually do (these frequently diverge—people often rationalize their actions after the fact in ways that Jangan match their real-time behavior)
- Emotional reactions (frustration, delight, confusion) that reveal experience quality beyond pure task completion rates

## **Dealing with Design Perfectionism vs Time Constraints**

This is one of the most common sources of genuine friction in the PM-Design relationship.

**The design perspective (often legitimate):** "This isn't ready yet. There are edge cases we haven't solved, the interaction feels clunky, and shipping this prematurely will create a worse user experience and more rework later."

**The PM perspective (often also legitimate):** "We have a real business deadline, a competitor is moving fast in this space, and 'good enough to ship and iterate on' is a reasonable, valid strategy—perfect can genuinely become the enemy of good in a competitive market."

**Both perspectives usually contain genuine truth.** The unhealthy pattern is either extreme: a PM who treats every design timeline concern as unnecessary excessive perfectionism ("just ship it, we'll fix it later" as a permanent excuse to skip genuine quality bar-setting), or a design culture where nothing ever feels "ready enough" to ship, creating chronic delay regardless of actual business urgency.

**A healthier framing:**

1. **Define what "good enough for v1" genuinely means together**, explicitly, rather than leaving it ambiguous and letting design and PM operate on different implicit standards. What specific edge cases must be handled before launch, versus which can genuinely wait for a fast-follow iteration?
    
2. **Distinguish between "this is imperfect" and "this is actually broken/harmful."** Imperfect but functional experiences can often reasonably ship with a plan for iteration. Actually broken or confusing experiences (that will generate support tickets, user frustration, or reputation damage) deserve a higher bar before shipping, regardless of timeline pressure.
    
3. **Commit genuinely to the "fast-follow" iteration**, not just as a rhetorical device to pressure design into shipping something imperfect, then quietly abandoning the promised follow-up work once attention moves to the next shiny priority. If design has agreed to ship v1 with known gaps based on your promise to address them soon, honor that commitment—breaking it repeatedly destroys the trust needed for this compromise to work in future situations.

## **Common PM Mistakes That Damage Design Relationships**

### **Dictating Solutions Daripada Sharing Problems**

We've covered this, but it's worth repeating because it's genuinely the most common mistake: coming to design with "build me this specific screen with these specific elements" Daripada "here's the masalah user and business constraint, let's explore the best solution together."

### **Ignoring Design Rationale**

When a designer explains _why_ they made a specific choice (based on established interaction patterns, cognitive load research, or specific usability testing insight), and a PM simply overrides it based on personal aesthetic preference ("I just Jangan like how that looks") without genuinely engaging with the underlying reasoning—this is deeply frustrating for designers who've invested real expertise into that decision.

**A healthier response to disagreement:** "Help me understand the reasoning behind this choice" before pushing your own preference, and if you still disagree after understanding their reasoning, articulate your counter-reasoning specifically (not just personal taste), inviting genuine dialogue rather than simply asserting authority to override their expert judgment.

### **Treating Design as a Production Function, Not a Strategic Partner**

Bringing design in only at the "we need mockups for this already-decided feature" stage, rather than involving them in earlier discovery and strategic conversations where their perspective on user experience implications could meaningfully shape the direction before it's already locked in.

### **Inconsistent Feedback**

Giving vague feedback ("this doesn't feel right") without specific, actionable reasoning, forcing designers to guess at what you actually mean and iterate blindly rather than addressing genuine, articulated concerns.

**A better feedback framework:** Be specific about the _problem_ you're perceiving (not the solution): "I'm concerned users might not notice this call-to-action given how similar it looks to the secondary buttons around it" rather than just "I Jangan like this button" or worse, prescribing your own specific alternative design without explaining the underlying concern driving that preference.

## **Building Genuine Design Partnership**

**Involve design early, genuinely, not just performatively.** If design is invited to discovery conversations but their input is consistently ignored in favor of decisions PM has essentially already made, this "involvement" becomes a hollow, resentment-generating ritual rather than genuine collaboration.

**Advocate for design's needs in resourcing and timeline conversations**, just as you would for engineering's. If design consistently gets compressed timelines because "engineering needs the specs" while design's own exploration and iteration time gets treated as expendable, this creates resentment and, predictably, worse outcomes.

**Respect design systems and established patterns**, rather than requesting bespoke, one-off solutions for every individual feature that Jangan consider the broader product's consistency and the genuine engineering/design cost of maintaining inconsistent patterns across the product.

**Give design genuine visibility into business context and constraints**, not just isolated tickets divorced from broader strategic reasoning. Designers make better decisions when they understand the "why" behind constraints, not just the constraints themselves as arbitrary limitations imposed from above.

## **When Design and Business Priorities Genuinely Conflict**

Sometimes design's recommendation for the best user experience genuinely conflicts with business needs (a design pattern that maximizes short-term conversion but that design believes creates a manipulative or poor long-term user experience, Contohnya,).

**This tension is often genuinely healthy**, not a problem to eliminate—design frequently serves as an important check against purely short-term business expedience overriding genuine user wellbeing (we'll explore this ethical tension deeply in Chapter 21 and Chapter 38).

**A healthy process for navigating this:**

1. **Take design's concern seriously as legitimate signal**, not just an obstacle to route around. If design is raising an ethical or long-term user trust concern, this deserves genuine consideration at an appropriate level, not just PM unilaterally overriding it because of short-term pressure.
    
2. **Bring the tension to the surface explicitly with relevant stakeholders**, rather than either PM unilaterally deciding, or the disagreement simmering unresolved between PM and design without broader visibility.
    
3. **When business genuinely needs to prioritize short-term metrics over design's ideal recommendation, be honest about this trade-off** rather than pretending the design concern wasn't valid. "We hear the concern about long-term trust; we're making a deliberate trade-off here for [specific reason], and we're planning to revisit/monitor for negative effects" is more honest and relationship-preserving than dismissing the concern outright.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Design isn't a decoration function—it's a discipline with genuine expertise, methodology, and rigorous thinking deserving the same respect and collaborative dialogue you'd extend to engineering's technical judgment.

PM should own defining the problem clearly; design should own exploring and determining the best solution, with genuine collaborative dialogue throughout rather than rigid, one-directional handoffs.

Navigate design perfectionism versus timeline pressure by explicitly defining what "good enough for v1" means together, distinguishing imperfect-but-functional from actually-broken experiences, and genuinely honoring fast-follow commitments rather than using them as empty rhetorical devices.

Avoid dictating solutions, ignoring design rationale, treating design as a pure production function, and giving vague, unactionable feedback—instead, involve design early and genuinely, advocate for their resourcing needs, and give them real business context to make better-informed decisions.
