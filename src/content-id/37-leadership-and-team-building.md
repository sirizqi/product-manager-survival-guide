## **The Worst Manager I Ever Became (Briefly)**

When I got my Pertama, management role—leading a team of four PMs—I approached it exactly the way I'd approached being an excellent individual contributor: work hard, have great answers, solve problems quickly, demonstrate expertise.

Within two months, one of my directs, a genuinely talented PM named Maya, asked for a 1:1 outside our normal cadence. She said, carefully, "I feel like every time I bring you a problem, you just solve it for me. I never get to actually work through it myself."

She was completely right, and it stung precisely because she'd caught me doing exactly what had made me successful as an IC: swooping in with the answer. Except now, my job wasn't to have the answer. My job was to help _her_ develop the ability to find answers herself, even when—especially when—that meant watching her struggle with something I could have solved in five minutes myself.

This is the single hardest transition in the entire PM career ladder: **moving from being evaluated on your own output to being evaluated on other people's growth and output.** The skills that made you an excellent PM (having sharp answers, moving fast, demonstrating expertise) can actively sabotage your effectiveness as a manager if you Jangan consciously unlearn some of these instincts and replace them with a completely different toolkit.

## **Leading Without Managing: The IC Leadership Path**

Not everyone wants (or should want) to manage people. Many Lead/Principal PMs exercise significant leadership without any direct reports.

**What leadership without formal authority looks like:**

**Setting direction through influence and expertise**, not through org chart position. Being the person whose product judgment is trusted enough that teams genuinely want your input on hard decisions, even though they Jangan report to you.

**Mentoring informally**, even without a formal reporting relationship. Junior PMs often learn as much (sometimes more) from senior IC peers who take genuine interest in their development as from their actual managers.

**Modeling excellent practice** that others observe and adopt. If you consistently write clear PRDs, run genuinely useful discovery, and communicate with exceptional clarity, this creates an implicit standard others aspire to match, without you ever explicitly "managing" anyone.

**Raising the bar through peer feedback and code review-style critique** of product work (reviewing others' PRDs, strategy docs, or research synthesis with genuine, constructive rigor)—this is a real form of leadership that doesn't require formal management authority.

## **Mentoring Junior PMs: The Art of Not Just Giving Answers**

Whether formally assigned or informally taken on, mentoring junior PMs is one of the most valuable (and most commonly done poorly) leadership activities available to experienced PMs.

**The instinct to resist:** Immediately solving the junior PM's problem for them, the way I did with Maya. This feels helpful in the moment (fast, efficient, demonstrates your expertise) but actively stunts their development over time.

**A better approach—coaching through questions, not answers:**

Daripada: "Here's exactly what Sebaiknya kamu do about this stakeholder conflict."

Try: "What have you tried so far? What do you think is actually driving their resistance? What options have you considered, and what makes you hesitant about each one?"

This takes more time and patience in the moment, but genuinely builds the junior PM's own problem-solving capability, rather than creating dependency on you as the answer-generator for every difficult situation they encounter.

**Balancing challenge and support:** Good mentorship isn't purely hands-off ("figure it out yourself, that's how you'll learn") nor purely hands-on (solving everything for them). It's calibrated challenge with genuine safety net support—letting them struggle productively with real problems while ensuring they're not left to fail catastrophically without any guidance or backup when genuinely needed.

**Sharing your own failures genuinely, not just polished success stories.** Junior PMs learn enormously from hearing about mistakes you made and what you learned, not just curated highlight reels of your successes. This also builds genuine trust and psychological safety for them to share their own struggles honestly, rather than performing false competence to avoid seeming inexperienced.

## **Building PM Team Culture (Beyond Just Individual Management)**

If you're managing multiple PMs, you're not just developing individuals—you're shaping a team culture that either reinforces or undermines good practice at scale.

**What healthy PM team culture looks like:**

**Genuine peer learning and knowledge-sharing**, not just top-down management directing individual PMs in isolation. Regular practices like PRD reviews among peers, shared research repositories, and open discussion of both successes and failures across the team.

**Psychological safety to admit uncertainty and mistakes.** If PMs on your team feel they must project false confidence and hide struggles or failures, you lose enormous learning opportunity and create a culture of performative competence over genuine growth.

**Consistent standards without rigid uniformity.** Shared templates, frameworks, and quality bars (for PRDs, research synthesis, roadmap communication) create consistency and mutual understanding, while still allowing individual PM judgment and style within that shared foundation.

**Genuine celebration of good process, not just good outcomes.** A PM who ran rigorous discovery, made a well-reasoned bet, and had it not work out due to genuinely unpredictable factors deserves recognition for the quality of their process, not just blanket disappointment based purely on the outcome—otherwise, you inadvertently train your team toward risk-aversion and outcome-gaming rather than genuine rigor.

**What unhealthy PM team culture looks like:**

- Internal competition and hoarding of information/relationships as personal career-advancement tactics
- Blame-oriented retrospectives when things go wrong, creating fear rather than genuine learning
- Wildly inconsistent standards across different PMs on the same team, creating confusion for engineering/design partners who work across multiple PMs
- Purely outcome-based evaluation that ignores process quality, inadvertently rewarding lucky gambles and punishing well-reasoned bets that didn't pan out

## **Hiring PMs: Red Flags in the Interview Process**

If you're involved in hiring decisions, here are patterns worth watching for that Jangan always show up clearly in a polished resume or rehearsed interview answers.

**Red flag: Can't articulate genuine failure or mistakes.** Candidates who present every past experience as a clean, unblemished success story either lack genuine self-awareness, aren't being honest, or haven't actually taken on sufficiently challenging, ambiguous work to have experienced meaningful failure. Genuine PM work involves regular failure and course-correction—someone who can't discuss this authentically is a concerning signal.

**Red flag: Blames others excessively for past struggles.** "Engineering was just bad" or "leadership didn't support me" as the sole explanation for past struggles, without any genuine self-reflection on their own contribution to the situation, suggests a pattern likely to repeat in your organization.

**Red flag: Can't explain their reasoning, only their conclusions.** If a candidate can tell you _what_ they decided but struggles to walk through genuine reasoning for _why_, this suggests either they didn't actually drive the strategic thinking themselves (perhaps overstating their role in past work), or they lack the reflective clarity needed for genuinely rigorous product thinking.

**Red flag: Treats every question as an opportunity to demonstrate frameworks rather than genuine thinking.** Candidates who respond to every case study question by immediately reciting a named framework (RICE! Kano! Jobs to Be Done!) without genuinely engaging with the specific nuance of the actual scenario presented often have more framework knowledge than genuine product judgment.

**Positive signal: Genuine curiosity during the interview itself.** Candidates who ask genuinely thoughtful, specific questions about your product, your users, your challenges—not just generic "what's the culture like" questions—demonstrate the kind of authentic curiosity that correlates strongly with good discovery instincts on the job.

**Positive signal: Comfortable saying "I Jangan know" or "I'd need to research this before being confident."** Especially in case study or hypothetical scenarios, candidates who acknowledge genuine uncertainty rather than confidently bluffing through unfamiliar territory demonstrate the intellectual honesty crucial for good product judgment.

## **Performance Management for PMs (Harder Than It Looks)**

PM performance is notoriously difficult to evaluate cleanly, since much of the job involves influence, judgment, and long-term strategic thinking that doesn't reduce easily to simple, objective metrics.

**Common performance management mistakes:**

**Evaluating purely on shipped output volume**, inadvertently incentivizing feature-factory behavior (shipping lots of small, low-impact things to look "productive") over genuinely impactful strategic work that might take longer to develop and show results.

**Evaluating purely on business outcomes**, ignoring the genuine influence of factors outside the PM's control (market conditions, other teams' execution, external competitive moves) that significantly affect outcomes regardless of the PM's individual judgment and effort quality.

**Vague, unclear expectations that only become clear retroactively** during a disappointing performance review, rather than being explicitly established and discussed proactively throughout the review period.

**A healthier approach to PM performance evaluation:**

**Evaluate process quality alongside outcomes.** Did this PM run genuine discovery, make well-reasoned strategic bets, communicate clearly, and navigate stakeholder complexity skillfully—regardless of whether the ultimate outcome was fully within their control? This doesn't mean outcomes Jangan matter at all, but pure outcome-based evaluation ignores enormous, legitimate variance from factors outside individual control.

**Set explicit expectations early and revisit them regularly**, not just during a formal annual/biannual review cycle. Regular 1:1 conversations about "here's what I'm seeing as strong, here's where I think there's room to grow" prevent performance conversations from becoming unexpected, retroactive surprises.

**Calibrate expectations to actual level and tenure.** A newer PM navigating their Pertama, major stakeholder conflict poorly is a genuine growth opportunity worth coaching through, not necessarily a performance failure—while the same struggle from a senior PM with years of experience might genuinely warrant more serious performance concern.

## **The Necessary Evil: Firing PMs (When It's Genuinely Warranted)**

This is uncomfortable to discuss, but it's a genuine, unavoidable part of management responsibility that deserves honest treatment rather than avoidance.

**When performance issues genuinely warrant termination (after appropriate support and coaching attempts have failed):**

**Be honest with yourself about whether you've genuinely provided adequate support and clear expectations Pertama,.** Sometimes underperformance is actually a management failure (unclear expectations, insufficient support, poor role fit) rather than a genuine individual performance problem—be honest about which situation you're actually in before proceeding toward termination.

**Document genuinely, not performatively.** Clear documentation of specific performance concerns, coaching provided, and expectations set isn't just legal protection—it's genuinely useful for ensuring you're being fair and accurate in your assessment, not acting on vague, unexamined frustration.

**When termination is genuinely necessary, handle it with dignity.** This is someone's livelihood and professional identity at stake. Clear, direct, compassionate communication—not vague euphemisms that leave someone confused about what actually happened, but also not needlessly harsh or humiliating in delivery.

**Jangan let fear of difficult conversations lead to indefinite avoidance.** Keeping someone in a role where they're genuinely struggling and unlikely to succeed, purely because the termination conversation feels uncomfortable, ultimately serves neither the individual (who deserves honest feedback and the opportunity to find a better-fitting role elsewhere) nor your team (who bears the burden of consistently covering for underperformance).

## **The Specific Challenge of Managing PMs Who Were Recently Your Peers**

A common, genuinely awkward scenario: you get promoted to manage PMs who were previously your peers, sometimes including people more experienced or tenured than you.

**Why this is genuinely hard:** Existing peer relationships and dynamics Jangan automatically adjust to the new authority structure. Former peers might resent the change, test boundaries, or simply feel awkward about the shifted dynamic, even with genuine goodwill on both sides.

**Practical navigation:**

**Have an explicit, honest conversation about the transition** rather than pretending it's not awkward or hoping it resolves itself silently over time. "I know this shift changes our dynamic somewhat—I want to talk openly about how we navigate this together" acknowledges reality directly rather than avoiding it.

**Resist the urge to either over-assert new authority** (proving you're now "the boss" through unnecessary displays of decision-making power) **or under-assert it** (avoiding any genuine management responsibility out of discomfort with the awkwardness, essentially abdicating the role you've been given).

**Be especially scrupulous about fairness** in this specific situation, since any perceived favoritism (toward or against former peers) will be scrutinized more heavily than it would be with a team you didn't have prior peer history with.

## **The Trap of "Player-Coach" Overextension**

Many PM managers, especially at smaller companies, are expected to both manage a team AND maintain significant individual contributor product responsibilities ("player-coach" roles). This is genuinely difficult to do well, and it's worth naming honestly rather than pretending it's a simple, sustainable arrangement.

**Why this is hard:** Management (genuine coaching, thoughtful hiring, culture-building, difficult performance conversations) requires real, protected time and attention. If you're simultaneously carrying a full individual contributor product workload, management responsibilities predictably get squeezed into whatever time remains—usually resulting in reactive, surface-level management rather than genuinely thoughtful development of your team.

**A more honest approach:** If you're genuinely in a player-coach role (common in smaller organizations where dedicated management bandwidth isn't yet feasible), be explicit with yourself and your organization about the trade-offs this creates, rather than pretending Kamu bisa excel equally at both without any compromise. Advocate proactively for reduced individual contributor scope as your management responsibilities genuinely grow, rather than silently absorbing an unsustainable combined workload until burnout forces the issue.
