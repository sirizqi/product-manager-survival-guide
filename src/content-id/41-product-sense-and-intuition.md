## **The Interview Question That Exposed Everything**

"Critique the checkout flow of this e-commerce app," the interviewer said, sliding a phone across the table during my Senior PM interview at a company I desperately wanted to join.

I launched into what I thought was an impressive analysis: I mentioned Hick's Law about choice overload, cited a Baymard Institute statistic about cart abandonment rates, and referenced three competitor checkout flows I'd studied. I sounded, I thought, extremely knowledgeable.

The interviewer nodded politely, then asked one simple question: "Okay, but what do YOU actually think is wrong with THIS specific flow, right in front of you?"

I froze. I'd been so busy performing "product sense" through borrowed frameworks and memorized statistics that I hadn't actually looked closely at the app in front of me. When I Terakhir, did—genuinely looked, rather than pattern-matching to prepared talking points—I noticed something real: the shipping cost was hidden until the very last step, creating exactly the kind of unpleasant surprise that tanks conversion. Simple. Specific. Actually observed, not recited from memory.

I didn't get that job (that specific stumble, among others, likely didn't help). But it taught me something crucial about the mysterious, often poorly-defined concept of "product sense": **it's not about how many frameworks Kamu bisa name or how much industry trivia you've memorized. It's about the ability to genuinely observe, deeply understand human behavior, and form independent, well-reasoned judgment—not performing borrowed expertise, but developing authentic pattern recognition earned through genuine attention and reflection.**

## **What Product Sense Actually Is (Demystified)**

"Product sense" gets thrown around so vaguely that it's become almost meaningless corporate jargon—something people claim to have or lack without ever precisely defining what it actually consists of. Let's fix that.

**Product sense is, at its core, a combination of:**

**1. Deep empathy for user psychology and behavior**—not just knowing research methodologies, but genuinely, intuitively understanding how humans think, feel, and behave when interacting with products, including behaviors people themselves might not consciously articulate or even be aware of.

**2. Pattern recognition built from extensive exposure**—having seen enough products, features, and user behaviors that Kamu bisa recognize recurring patterns (this interaction pattern tends to confuse users; this onboarding approach tends to work well for this type of complexity) without needing to consciously re-derive Pertama, principles every single time.

**3. Aesthetic and interaction judgment**—a genuine, cultivated sense for what feels intuitive, delightful, or frustrating in an interface or experience, going beyond pure functional logic into the realm of genuine craft sensibility.

**4. Business and strategic context integration**—understanding not just what would be ideal for users in a vacuum, but Cara balance genuine user needs against business constraints and strategic priorities in a way that produces sustainable, viable products.

**None of this is magic or innate talent exclusively.** It's built through deliberate practice, genuine curiosity, and consistent reflection—which means it's absolutely learnable and improvable, contrary to the mystical way "product sense" often gets discussed as some rare, unteachable gift certain lucky people simply possess.

## **Taste vs Data: The Balancing Act**

Here's a genuine tension experienced PMs navigate constantly: sometimes your cultivated "taste" (product sense) points in a different direction than available data, and knowing which to trust—and when—is itself a crucial skill.

**When taste should lead:**

- Early-stage, pre-data situations where you simply Jangan have enough usage volume for statistical confidence
- Genuinely novel product categories where historical patterns and existing data Jangan clearly apply
- Aesthetic and craft decisions where "does this feel good to use" matters more than any quantifiable metric could fully capture

**When data should lead:**

- You have genuinely adequate sample size and statistical confidence
- Your personal taste might be systematically unrepresentative of your actual user base (a common trap: PMs designing for themselves rather than for their actual, often quite different, target users)
- The stakes are high enough that intuition-based mistakes would be genuinely costly

**The healthiest integration:** Use product sense to generate hypotheses and identify what's worth testing, then use data to validate or challenge those hypotheses rather than treating either taste or data as an infallible oracle that eliminates the need for the other. The best PMs I know have strong intuitions AND genuine intellectual humility about being wrong, actively seeking disconfirming data rather than just confirmation of their existing instincts.

## **Deconstructing Great Products: A Practice, Not Just a Skill**

Here's something almost every genuinely strong product person does, whether formally or informally: they constantly, deliberately deconstruct products they encounter in daily life—not just competitor products in their specific industry, but genuinely everything.

**The practice of product teardown:**

When you use any product—an app, a physical device, even a well-designed restaurant menu—pause and genuinely ask:

- What specific user need is this addressing, and how well does it actually address it?
- What deliberate trade-offs were made here, and why might the designers have made those specific choices?
- What would I have done differently, and why do I think my approach would be better (or would it actually be worse, upon genuine reflection)?
- What's genuinely delightful here that I could learn from, regardless of industry relevance?
- What's genuinely frustrating here, and what does that frustration teach me about a pattern to avoid?

**Why cross-industry observation matters:** Some of the most genuinely innovative product thinking comes from applying patterns observed in completely unrelated industries to your own specific context. A subscription box service might learn something valuable from studying how a great restaurant manages the pacing and reveal of a tasting menu. A B2B SaaS onboarding flow might learn something from studying how a well-designed video game teaches new mechanics without overwhelming players.

**Making this a genuine daily practice:** This isn't about becoming an insufferable person who can't simply enjoy using products without constant analysis. It's about developing a habit of _occasional_, genuine curiosity—noticing patterns, forming hypotheses about why specific choices were made, and slowly building an internal library of observed patterns that compounds into genuine intuition over time.

## **The Difference Between Genuine Product Sense and Performed Confidence**

This distinction matters enormously, and it's exactly what tripped me up in that interview story above.

**Performed confidence sounds like:** Confidently citing frameworks, statistics, and best practices without genuinely engaging with the specific nuance of the actual situation in front of you. It's pattern-matching to "sounds like an expert" rather than genuinely thinking through the specific case.

**Genuine product sense sounds like:** Being willing to say "I'm not sure, let me actually look closely at this specific situation" before forming a judgment, then forming that judgment based on actual, specific observation rather than generic, borrowed expertise that might not actually apply to this particular case.

**A useful self-check:** When you form a product opinion, ask yourself honestly: "Am I saying this because I've genuinely thought through this specific situation, or because it's a comfortable, familiar-sounding thing to say that demonstrates I know relevant frameworks and industry patterns?" The former is genuine product sense being exercised; the latter is performance that might occasionally happen to be correct, but isn't built on the kind of authentic reasoning that reliably produces good judgment across novel situations.

## **Building Pattern Recognition Deliberately (Not Just Passively Hoping It Develops)**

Since product sense is built through exposure and reflection, Kamu bisa accelerate its development through deliberate practice, rather than just passively hoping years of experience eventually produce good intuition.

**Practical exercises:**

**1. Daily product teardown (as described above), but actually written down.** Keep a simple running notes document where you jot down specific observations, not just mental notes that evaporate without ever being genuinely processed and retained.

**2. Actively study genuinely excellent and genuinely poor examples side by side.** Jangan just study products you personally admire—deliberately examine products you find frustrating or poorly designed, and articulate specifically _why_, not just "this feels bad." Specificity is what builds transferable pattern recognition, not vague dissatisfaction.

**3. Predict before you observe.** Before looking at actual user research or usage data for a feature, write down your genuine prediction of what you expect to find. Then compare your prediction against actual results. This calibration exercise, done consistently over time, is one of the most powerful ways to genuinely improve intuition—you learn specifically where your instincts are reliable and where they systematically mislead you.

**4. Seek out genuinely diverse product categories, not just your own industry.** If you work in B2B SaaS, deliberately spend time using and studying consumer mobile apps, physical retail experiences, even non-digital product design (genuinely well-designed physical tools, packaging, architecture). Cross-domain pattern recognition often produces more genuinely novel insight than narrow, single-industry expertise.

**5. Discuss and debate product observations with genuinely thoughtful peers.** Product sense develops faster through genuine dialogue and respectful disagreement with people who see things differently than you, rather than purely solo reflection that risks reinforcing your own existing blind spots without external challenge.

## **The Trap of Mistaking Personal Preference for Universal Product Sense**

Here's a genuinely important, humbling caveat: your own personal product taste and preferences are not automatically representative of your target users, and mistaking personal preference for genuine product sense is one of the most common failure modes among PMs who are otherwise quite talented.

**A classic example:** A PM who personally loves minimalist, feature-sparse interfaces might consistently push products toward extreme simplicity, genuinely believing this reflects sound product judgment—when their actual target users (perhaps power users in a complex B2B tool) might genuinely prefer more visible functionality and information density than the PM's personal aesthetic preference would naturally suggest.

**The fix:** Genuine product sense includes explicit awareness of whose perspective you're actually channeling. Are you predicting how your _actual target users_ (based on genuine research and observation of them specifically) would respond, or are you simply projecting your own personal taste and calling it "product sense"? This distinction requires genuine intellectual honesty and active effort to separate—it's not automatic, even for experienced PMs.

## **When Product Sense Should Yield to Genuine User Research**

Product sense is genuinely valuable, but it's not infallible, and knowing when to trust your cultivated intuition versus when to seek genuine external validation is itself part of mature product judgment.

**Situations where Sebaiknya kamu distrust your own product sense more:**

- You're designing for a user demographic or context genuinely different from your own lived experience (a young tech-savvy PM designing for elderly users unfamiliar with technology, Contohnya,)
- The stakes of being wrong are genuinely high (a major strategic bet, not a minor UI tweak)
- You notice you're feeling unusually certain and resistant to considering alternative perspectives (overconfidence is often a warning sign, not a genuine signal of correctness)
- Multiple people you respect are expressing genuine skepticism about your instinct

**In these situations, lean more heavily on genuine discovery and research (Chapter 4) rather than trusting pure intuition, even well-cultivated intuition, to reliably guide high-stakes decisions in unfamiliar territory.**
