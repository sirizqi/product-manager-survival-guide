## **The Meeting Where I Learned I Had No Power**

Early in my PM career, I walked into a meeting with a clear plan: convince the tim engineering to prioritize a refactor I believed was critical. I'd prepared my case. I had data. I had conviction. I said, "I need this done by end of quarter."

The engineering lead looked at me, not unkindly, and said, "You need it, or the business needs it? And either way, that's not really how this works—we Jangan report to you."

I remember the specific feeling of that moment: a sudden, uncomfortable realization that despite my title, despite my "ownership" of the product, I had precisely zero formal authority to make anyone do anything. I could write documents. I could make requests. I could escalate. But I could not simply _order_ engineering to do something and expect compliance the way a manager could with a direct report.

This is, without exaggeration, one of the most disorienting realizations for new PMs, especially those coming from more traditionally hierarchical roles or industries. **You are responsible for outcomes you cannot directly control, executed by people who Jangan report to you, using resources you Jangan personally allocate.** If this sounds like a recipe for chronic frustration, that's because it absolutely can be—unless you develop genuine skill in influence, which is fundamentally different from authority and requires an entirely different toolkit.

## **Why PMs (Almost) Never Have Direct Authority**

This isn't an accident or an organizational design flaw—it's actually intentional in most well-functioning companies, for good reason.

**If PMs had direct authority over engineers and designers:**

- Engineering and design would lose the professional autonomy that (research consistently shows) drives higher quality work and job satisfaction in creative, technical fields
- PMs would be incentivized to demand unrealistic scope/timelines without facing the direct consequences engineering faces for unsustainable pace
- Cross-functional expertise would get flattened into a single point of authority, losing the valuable check-and-balance of multiple disciplines weighing in on decisions

**The trade-off:** You get broader scope and strategic influence in exchange for giving up direct command authority. This is a reasonable trade for the organization, even though it can feel personally frustrating when you're the one navigating it day to day.

## **The Psychology of Persuasion (Ethically Applied)**

Robert Cialdini's classic research on influence identifies several principles that, applied ethically, genuinely help you build the kind of influence PMs need daily.

### **Reciprocity**

People are more inclined to help those who've helped them Pertama,. This doesn't mean transactional keeping-score—it means genuinely investing in relationships and offering help before you need anything in return.

**Practical application:** Before asking engineering for a favor (rushing a fix, taking on extra scope), have you actually helped them with something Pertama,? Advocated for their technical debt priorities? Shielded them from unnecessary stakeholder noise? Genuine goodwill, built over time, makes future asks land very differently than cold transactional requests.

### **Social Proof**

People look to others' behavior (especially respected peers) to guide their own decisions, particularly under uncertainty.

**Practical application:** If you're trying to build momentum for an idea, having a respected senior engineer or designer publicly support it carries far more weight than you repeating your own argument louder. Identify who your specific audience respects, and find genuine ways to involve or reference their perspective.

### **Consistency and Commitment**

People feel psychological pressure to act consistently with commitments they've already made, especially public ones.

**Practical application:** Getting explicit, even small, buy-in early in a process ("does this direction make sense so far?") makes it more likely people stay genuinely aligned as the initiative progresses, rather than discovering disagreement much later when it's more costly to address.

### **Authority (The Ethical Version)**

People are more persuaded by perceived genuine expertise—not manufactured authority, but demonstrated competence and credibility built over time.

**Practical application:** This is why track record matters so much for PM influence (as discussed in Chapter 2). Each well-reasoned, accurate past decision builds the kind of earned credibility that makes future recommendations land with more weight, without needing to assert formal power you Jangan actually have.

**An important ethical line:** Cialdini's principles can absolutely be misused manipulatively (manufacturing false scarcity, fake social proof, exploiting reciprocity cynically). The ethical application of influence means genuinely helping people make good decisions with accurate information, not tricking people into decisions that serve you at their expense. If you find yourself using these principles to obscure rather than clarify, or to benefit yourself at genuine cost to others, you've crossed from influence into manipulation.

## **Building Credibility and Trust (The Slow, Unglamorous Work)**

There's no shortcut here, despite what LinkedIn "influence hacks" might promise. Genuine influence is built through consistent, unglamorous behavior over time:

**Being right more often than not.** Not perfect—nobody is—but demonstrating a track record of sound judgment, especially on things that were genuinely uncertain at the time you made the call.

**Following through on commitments.** If you say you'll get back to someone by Thursday, do it. If circumstances change and Kamu bisa't, communicate proactively rather than silently missing the deadline. Small broken commitments compound into significant credibility damage over time.

**Admitting when you're wrong, quickly and without excessive drama.** Paradoxically, people trust you _more_, not less, when Kamu bisa say "I was wrong about that, here's what I've learned" rather than defensively justifying a clearly mistaken position.

**Being consistent, not just agreeable.** If you tell different stakeholders different things depending on what they want to hear, this eventually surfaces (people talk to each other) and destroys trust rapidly and often irreparably.

**Showing genuine curiosity about others' expertise**, not just deploying them as means to your own ends. Engineers and designers can tell the difference between a PM who genuinely wants to understand their perspective and one who's just going through the motions before pushing their predetermined agenda anyway.

## **Negotiation Basics for PMs**

Much of PM work is, functionally, ongoing negotiation—even when it doesn't feel like a formal "negotiation" in the traditional sense.

### **Understand Interests, Not Just Positions**

**Position:** What someone says they want ("I need this feature built exactly this way")

**Interest:** The underlying need driving that position ("I need to close this deal, and the customer mentioned this specific gap")

Negotiating purely at the position level often creates unnecessary conflict, because positions can feel rigid and non-negotiable. Understanding underlying interests frequently reveals multiple possible solutions that satisfy the real need without requiring exactly the originally stated position.

**Practical application:** When engineering says "we can't do this," dig into why. Sometimes it's a genuine technical constraint. Sometimes it's actually "we Jangan have confidence in the requirements" or "we're worried about scope creep based on past experience with similar asks"—addressable concerns entirely different from an immovable technical wall.

### **Look for Win-Win, But Accept When It's Genuinely Not Available**

Not every negotiation has a perfect win-win solution. Sometimes there's a genuine trade-off where someone doesn't get what they initially wanted. Pretending otherwise, or manufacturing a false "everyone wins" narrative when it's not actually true, damages trust for future negotiations far more than honestly acknowledging a real trade-off.

### **The Power of "Help Me Understand"**

When facing pushback or disagreement, resist the instinct to immediately defend your position. Instead: "Help me understand your concern here" opens genuine dialogue, often surfacing information that changes your own thinking, or at minimum, helps you address the actual underlying objection rather than a assumed one.

## **Dealing with Pushback from Engineering and Design**

This deserves specific attention because it's one of the most common sources of PM frustration.

### **When Engineering Says "That's Not Possible"**

**Resist the reflexive reaction to argue or escalate immediately.** Pertama,, genuinely understand: is this a hard technical constraint, an estimate of significant effort/complexity, or a values-based objection (they think it's a bad idea, separate from technical feasibility)?

**These require completely different responses:**

- **Hard constraint:** Understand it fully, then explore genuinely different approaches to the underlying problem
- **Effort/complexity estimate:** This is really a prioritization conversation in disguise—is the effort worth the value, and what could be descoped?
- **Values-based objection:** This deserves genuine engagement, not dismissal. Sometimes engineers see problems (technical debt implications, user experience concerns, edge cases) that aren't visible from a purely Product Management vantage point.

### **When Design Pushes Back on Business Constraints**

Designers sometimes push back when business or timeline constraints conflict with what they believe creates the best user experience. This tension is often genuinely healthy—it's a check against pure business expedience overriding real user needs.

**A useful framing:** "I hear that this timeline/constraint isn't ideal for the experience you're envisioning. Here's the specific business reason this constraint exists [be honest and specific, not vague]. Given that constraint, what's the best version of this experience we can create together?" This acknowledges their expertise and concern while being transparent about genuine constraints, rather than either dismissing their input or pretending constraints Jangan exist.

## **Avoiding Passive-Aggressive Behavior and Playing Victim**

Here's an uncomfortable pattern worth naming directly: when PMs feel powerless (lacking formal authority) but frustrated (genuinely needing things to happen), it's tempting to slip into unproductive behaviors:

**Passive-aggressive escalation:** Daripada directly addressing a disagreement with the person involved, complaining about them to their manager, or venting in Slack channels where the person isn't present. This poisons relationships and rarely resolves the actual underlying issue.

**Playing victim:** Framing every obstacle as "engineering/design/leadership won't let me do my job," rather than taking ownership of your own influence-building responsibility. This might generate temporary sympathy, but it doesn't actually solve problems, and repeated victim-framing damages your credibility as someone capable of navigating organizational complexity (a core PM skill, as we've established).

**The healthier alternative:** Direct, honest conversation with the actual person involved, focused on understanding their perspective and finding a genuine path forward—even when this feels less immediately satisfying than venting frustration to a sympathetic Ketiga, party.

## **The "Bank Account" Model of Organizational Capital**

Here's a mental model that helps calibrate how much Kamu bisa reasonably ask of people who Jangan report to you: think of your relationship with each key stakeholder as a bank account of trust and goodwill.

**Deposits (building the account):**

- Delivering on commitments reliably
- Advocating for their priorities/concerns when relevant
- Giving credit generously
- Being genuinely helpful without expecting immediate reciprocity
- Communicating clearly and honestly, especially during difficulty

**Withdrawals (spending the account):**

- Asking for rushed work or exceptions to normal process
- Pushing back on their expertise or judgment
- Requesting they deprioritize their own priorities for yours
- Any ask that requires extra effort, stress, or inconvenience on their part

**The key insight:** Kamu bisa absolutely make withdrawals sometimes—that's normal and healthy in any working relationship. But if you're constantly withdrawing without ever depositing, the account goes negative, and people become far less willing to accommodate your asks, regardless of how reasonable any individual request might be in isolation.

**A practical check-in question:** For your most important cross-functional relationships, honestly assess: am I currently running a positive or negative balance? If negative, what deposits could you make before your next significant ask?
