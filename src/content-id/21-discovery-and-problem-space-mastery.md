## **The Interview That Taught Me Nothing (And I Thought It Taught Me Everything)**

Early in my career, I conducted what I proudly called "extensive user research." Ten interviews. I had a script. I asked questions. I took notes. I made a beautiful synthesis deck with sticky notes color-coded by theme (very Design Thinking™ of me).

The conclusion? Users desperately wanted the feature I'd already decided to build.

What a coincidence, right?

Looking back, here's what actually happened: I asked leading questions like "Would you find it useful if we added X?" (Everyone says yes to hypothetical features—it's free to say yes). I recruited users who already loved our product (survivorship bias, baby). I interpreted vague, polite feedback as enthusiastic validation ("that sounds interesting" ≠ "I would pay for this"). And I definitely didn't talk to anyone who'd churned or rejected us, because that felt "less relevant."

We built the feature. Nobody used it. Six months of engineering time, gone. My "extensive user research" hadn't researched anything—it had performed an elaborate ritual to make myself feel confident about a decision I'd already made.

This, my friends, is the most common sin in Product Management: **doing research to confirm what you already believe, dressed up in the language of objectivity.**

Let's fix that.

## **The Difference Between Research and Theater**

Here's an uncomfortable truth: most "user research" happening in companies right now is theater. It looks like research. It has the props of research (interviews! surveys! personas!). But it functions as validation theater, designed to make stakeholders feel good about decisions that have already been made.

**Research Theater looks like:**

- Interviewing 5 users, all recruited because they're "power users" who already love you
- Asking "Do you like this feature?" (nobody says no to your face)
- Running a survey with leading questions ("How excited are you about our amazing new feature?")
- Doing research _after_ the roadmap is already locked, just to check a box
- Presenting only the insights that support your existing hypothesis

**Actual Discovery looks like:**

- Talking to people who represent the full spectrum: happy users, frustrated users, users who churned, users who never converted
- Asking about their behavior and past experiences, not hypothetical future preferences
- Being genuinely willing to discover your idea is wrong
- Doing this _before_ you've committed resources, not as a rubber stamp
- Actively looking for disconfirming evidence, not just supporting quotes

The difference isn't the format—it's the intent. Are you trying to learn, or are you trying to be right?

## **Jobs to Be Done: Stop Asking What They Want, Start Asking Why**

Here's a framework that will change how you think about discovery forever: **Jobs to Be Done (JTBD)**.

The core insight is deceptively simple: people Jangan buy products, they "hire" products to do a job in their life. Understanding the _job_, not the product feature, is where real insight lives.

The classic example (you've probably heard this one, but it's a classic for a reason): McDonald's noticed milkshake sales spiking in the morning. Traditional research asked, "How can we make a better milkshake?" and got responses about flavor, thickness, sweetness—incremental nonsense.

But when researchers actually watched _who_ was buying milkshakes and _when_, they found commuters buying them alone, early morning, for their long, boring commute. The "job" wasn't "satisfy my milkshake craving." The job was "give me something filling and entertaining to do with one hand while I drive for 40 minutes."

Suddenly, the competition isn't other milkshakes—it's bananas, bagels, and boredom itself. That's a completely different strategi produk.

**Cara apply JTBD in your interviews:**

Daripada: _"What features would you like to see?"_

Ask: _"Walk me through the last time you tried to solve this problem. What were you doing right before? What triggered the need? What did you try Pertama,? Why didn't that work?"_

You're excavating the _story_ behind the behavior, not collecting a feature wishlist.

## **Steve Blank's Customer Development: Get Out of the Building**

Steve Blank's foundational insight for startups (that applies at any company size) is brutally simple: **there are no facts inside your building, only opinions.**

Your tim engineering has opinions about what users want. Your tim sales has opinions. Your CEO definitely has opinions (usually the loudest ones). None of these are facts until validated with actual users, in actual context, doing actual behavior.

**The Customer Development process:**

1. **Customer Discovery** – Do people actually have the problem you think they have?
2. **Customer Validation** – Will they actually pay for/use your proposed solution?
3. **Customer Creation** – Can you scale demand?
4. **Company Building** – Can you scale operations to meet that demand?

Most PMs skip straight to building without validating step 1 or 2. This is like building a house without checking if the land can support a foundation. Sure, it might work out. It also might sink into a sinkhole in year two.

## **The Deadly Sins of Discovery (Confessions Included)**

### **Sin #1: Asking Leading Questions**

**What it sounds like:** "Jangan you think it would be helpful if we added dark mode?"

**Why it's toxic:** You've told them the answer you want. Politeness (and sometimes exhaustion with the interview) will make most people agree with leading suggestions, even if they wouldn't actually use the feature.

**The fix:** "Tell me about your experience using the app at night" or "What's frustrating about using this in low-light conditions?" Let them bring up the pain point—Jangan hand it to them.

### **Sin #2: Research for Validation, Not Exploration**

**What it sounds like:** You've already decided to build Feature X. You do "research" purely to gather quotes that justify the decision to skeptical stakeholders.

**Why it's toxic:** This isn't research—it's theater with a research costume. You're not open to being wrong, so you won't see disconfirming evidence even when it's staring at you.

**The fix:** Go into discovery genuinely willing to kill your own idea. If Kamu bisa't imagine walking away from this feature based on what you learn, you're not actually doing discovery.

### **Sin #3: Confusing Customer Requests with Customer Needs**

**What it sounds like:** A customer says, "I need a way to export this to Excel." You build an Excel export feature.

**Why it's toxic:** Customers are experts in their problems, not in solutions. The customer might actually need "a way to share this data with my boss who only trusts spreadsheets," and Excel export is just the Pertama, solution they thought of. Maybe a better dashboard link would solve it faster.

**The fix:** Always dig one level deeper. "What would you do with that Excel export once you had it?" Sometimes the answer reveals a completely different (and better) solution.

### **Sin #4: Small Sample, Big Conclusions**

**What it sounds like:** "I talked to 3 users and they all said X, so we should definitely build X for all 50,000 users."

**Why it's toxic:** Qualitative research (interviews) is great for understanding depth and generating hypotheses, but terrible for statistical confidence. Three enthusiastic users Jangan represent your entire user base—they might just be three unusually vocal people.

**The fix:** Use qualitative research to generate hypotheses, then validate at scale with quantitative data (surveys, usage analytics, A/B tests) before making big bets.

### **Sin #5: Only Talking to Happy Users**

**What it sounds like:** Recruiting from your "power user" newsletter list or asking your Customer Success team to "send over some happy clients for feedback."

**Why it's toxic:** You're selecting for people who already love you. You'll never uncover why people churn, why prospects Jangan convert, or what's actually broken, because you've curated a bubble of positivity.

**The fix:** Deliberately include churned users, users who never activated, and users who complained in support tickets. The uncomfortable feedback is usually the most valuable feedback.

## **The Discovery Toolkit: What Actually Works**

### **1. The Problem Interview**

Purpose: Understand if a problem is real, painful, and worth solving.

**Structure:**

- Open with context-setting questions about their role/situation
- Ask about past behavior related to the problem area (not hypotheticals)
- Probe for emotional intensity ("How did that make you feel? What did you do next?")
- Ask about workarounds they've already tried (this reveals how much pain they're actually experiencing)
- Never mention your proposed solution until the very end, if at all

### **2. The Solution Interview**

Purpose: Once you've confirmed the problem is real, test if your proposed solution actually addresses it.

**Structure:**

- Present a low-fidelity prototype or concept (not a polished mockup—too polished makes people hesitant to criticize)
- Ask them to think aloud as they interact with it
- Watch for confusion, hesitation, or workarounds they invent on the spot
- Ask "What would need to be true for you to switch from your current solution to this?"

### **3. Contextual Inquiry (Watching, Not Just Asking)**

Purpose: Observe actual behavior in the natural environment, because people are notoriously bad at accurately reporting their own behavior.

**Why this matters:** People will tell you they check email three times a day. Screen recordings will show them checking it every four minutes. The gap between self-reported and actual behavior is enormous, and contextual inquiry closes that gap.

### **4. The Five Whys**

Purpose: Dig past surface-level answers to root causes.

**Example:**

- "Why do you use spreadsheets for this?" → "Because our current tool doesn't do X."
- "Why do you need X?" → "Because I need to share data with my manager."
- "Why does your manager need it that way?" → "Because they Jangan trust the dashboard numbers."
- "Why Jangan they trust the dashboard?" → "Because it's been wrong before."
- "Why was it wrong before?" → _Ah, now we've found the real problem: data integrity issues, not export functionality._

### **5. Diary Studies**

Purpose: Capture behavior and feelings over time, especially for infrequent or emotionally complex behaviors that Jangan fit into a single interview session.

**Use case:** Understanding a multi-week purchasing decision, or tracking how frustration builds over repeated interactions with a broken workflow.

## **Insight Synthesis: Turning Chaos into Clarity**

You've done fifteen interviews. You have pages of notes, half-formed theories, and sticky notes multiplying like rabbits. Now what?

**Step 1: Look for patterns, not just quotes.** One person saying something interesting is an anecdote. Three or more people independently expressing the same underlying need (even with different words) is a pattern worth investigating.

**Step 2: Separate what they said from what it means.** "I wish this was faster" (what they said) might actually mean "I Jangan trust that my action was successful" (what it means). Jangan take statements at literal face value—dig into intent.

**Step 3: Build a research repository, not a one-off deck.** Insights decay if they live in a single Google Slides deck that gets buried in Drive after one presentation. Build a searchable repository (Dovetail, Notion, even a well-organized spreadsheet) so future-you and your teammates can reference past learnings Daripada re-discovering the same insights every quarter.

**Step 4: Present tension, not just conclusions.** Good synthesis doesn't just say "users want X." It says "Users in segment A want X, but users in segment B actively avoid X because of Y—here's the trade-off we need to navigate." This nuance is what separates useful research from oversimplified talking points.

## **When Discovery Becomes an Excuse for Inaction**

Let's balance the scales here, because there's an opposite failure mode: **analysis paralysis disguised as rigor**.

Some PMs use "we need more research" as a permanent shield against making decisions. They'll run interview after interview, survey after survey, always finding a reason why they need "just a bit more data" before committing to a direction.

At some point, you have enough signal to make an informed bet. Perfect certainty doesn't exist in product work—you're making probabilistic decisions with incomplete information, always. The goal of discovery isn't to eliminate all uncertainty; it's to reduce it to a level where the risk of moving forward is smaller than the risk of continuing to wait.

**A rough heuristic:** If your last three interviews aren't teaching you anything new (you're hearing the same patterns repeat), you've likely reached saturation. Time to act on what you've learned, not keep researching indefinitely.

## **A Discovery Framework Kamu bisa Actually Use Tomorrow**

Here's a simplified process Kamu bisa implement immediately:

1. **Define your riskiest assumption.** What's the one thing that, if wrong, would completely invalidate this idea?
2. **Design the smallest possible test to validate/invalidate that assumption.** This might be 5 interviews, a landing page test, or a fake-door experiment.
3. **Recruit for diversity, not comfort.** Include skeptics, churned users, and edge cases—not just your biggest fans.
4. **Ask about behavior and history, not hypothetical preferences.** "What did you do?" beats "What would you do?" every time.
5. **Actively hunt for disconfirming evidence.** If you're not even a little bit worried your idea might be wrong, you're not looking hard enough.
6. **Synthesize with your team, not alone.** Different people notice different patterns. Solo synthesis is prone to confirmation bias.
7. **Document and share, even if the answer is "this idea doesn't work."** Negative results are still valuable insights—they save future time and resources.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Most "user research" happening in companies is theater designed to validate decisions already made, not genuine discovery. Real discovery means being willing to be wrong—not just performing objectivity while chasing confirmation.

Jobs to Be Done and Customer Development frameworks force you to focus on actual behavior and root causes, not surface-level request fitur or hypothetical preferences.

Avoid the deadly sins: leading questions, cherry-picked happy users, small samples treated as gospel, and confusing requests with needs. But also avoid the opposite trap—using "more research needed" as a permanent excuse to avoid making decisions.
