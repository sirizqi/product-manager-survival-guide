## **The Onboarding Flow Nobody Questioned for Three Years**

When I joined a mid-stage startup as a Senior PM, I inherited an onboarding flow that had been essentially unchanged for three years. Seven steps, in a specific order, with specific copy. When I asked why it was structured this way, I got a series of answers that all amounted to the same thing: "That's just how it's always been."

Nobody could tell me the original reasoning. Nobody could point to research that validated the seven-step structure. It had simply calcified into "the way things are done here"—inherited by each subsequent PM as an unquestioned artifact, maintained through inertia rather than genuine ongoing validation.

When we Terakhir, did a Pertama,-principles teardown—stripping away the assumption that the existing structure was correct and instead asking "if we were designing this from scratch today, knowing what we now know about our users, what would we actually build?"—we discovered the seven-step flow had accumulated at least three steps that no longer served any genuine purpose (holdovers from features we'd since deprecated) and was missing crucial steps addressing friction points we'd only recently understood through more recent research.

We cut it to four steps, redesigned based on current understanding rather than historical accident, and saw completion rates improve by 34%.

**The lesson here isn't "always redesign everything from scratch."** It's that unexamined inheritance of "how things are done" is one of the most common, invisible ways that mediocre product decisions calcify into permanent fixtures, immune from genuine scrutiny simply because nobody bothers to ask "wait, why do we actually do it this way?"

## **Pertama, Principles Thinking: Beyond the Elon Musk Cliché**

Pertama, principles thinking has become somewhat cliché through overuse (thanks largely to its association with Elon Musk's public commentary), but the underlying method remains genuinely powerful when actually applied rather than just referenced as a buzzword.

**The core method:**

1. **Identify and explicitly state the assumptions underlying current practice**, rather than treating them as invisible, unquestioned background facts.
    
2. **Break the problem down to fundamental truths**—what do we actually, verifiably know to be true, separate from convention, assumption, or "how it's always been done"?
    
3. **Reason upward from those fundamentals** to construct a solution, rather than starting from the existing solution and making incremental adjustments.
    

**Practical example (rocket costs, Musk's own classic illustration):** Daripada asking "how can we make rockets slightly cheaper than current rocket manufacturers charge?" (reasoning by analogy to existing convention), ask "what are rockets actually made of, and what do those raw materials actually cost?" This reveals that the raw materials constitute a small fraction of typical rocket costs—meaning the "convention" of expensive rockets isn't a fundamental physical constraint, but an artifact of how the existing industry happens to operate, which opens space for fundamentally different approaches.

**Applying this to product decisions:** Daripada asking "how can we make our onboarding flow slightly better than it currently is?" (reasoning by analogy to your existing structure), ask "what does a new user fundamentally need to understand and experience to get genuine value from our product as quickly as possible?" This might reveal that your entire existing structure, not just its individual steps, deserves genuine reconsideration.

## **Reasoning by Analogy vs Reasoning by Pertama, Principles**

**Reasoning by analogy** (the default mode most people, most of the time, naturally use): "Competitor X does onboarding this way, and they seem successful, so we should do something similar." Or: "We've always structured our pricing this way, so any new pricing decision should follow that same basic pattern."

**Why analogy-based reasoning is genuinely useful, most of the time:** It's fast, requires less cognitive effort, and often produces reasonably good results because much of accumulated convention does reflect genuine, hard-won learning, not pure arbitrary historical accident. Kamu nggak perlu Pertama,-principles-reason your way through every single decision—that would be exhausting and often unnecessary.

**When reasoning by analogy becomes genuinely dangerous:** When the underlying context has meaningfully changed (your user base, technology capabilities, or competitive landscape looks fundamentally different than when the convention was originally established), or when you're trying to achieve genuinely differentiated, breakthrough results rather than simply keeping pace with existing convention.

**A useful practical heuristic:** Default to reasoning by analogy for lower-stakes, well-understood decisions (this saves genuine time and cognitive energy for where it matters more). Deliberately shift to Pertama,-principles reasoning for high-stakes strategic decisions, or whenever you notice yourself defending a choice purely with "that's how it's always been done" without being able to articulate genuine underlying reasoning.

## **Challenging Assumptions: A Practical Discipline**

Here's a genuinely useful habit: whenever you're working through a significant product decision, explicitly write out the assumptions you're operating under, then deliberately interrogate each one.

**A practical assumption-mapping exercise:**

Take a current initiative or long-standing practice. List every assumption embedded in it, including ones that feel so obvious they barely seem worth stating:

_Example (redesigning a checkout flow):_

- Assumption: Users complete checkout on the same device where they started browsing (is this actually true, or do significant numbers of users switch devices mid-journey?)
- Assumption: Users want to create an account before purchasing (or would a genuinely frictionless guest checkout option increase conversion, even if it complicates our post-purchase engagement strategy?)
- Assumption: More payment options are always better (or does excessive choice actually create decision paralysis and increase abandonment past a certain point?)
- Assumption: Our current checkout steps are in the optimal order (or has this order simply never been genuinely tested against alternatives?)

**For each assumption, ask:** Do we actually have evidence this is true, or have we simply accepted it as "obviously" correct without genuine scrutiny? This exercise routinely surfaces embedded assumptions that, once explicitly named, look far less obviously correct than they felt when operating invisibly in the background.

## **Avoiding Cargo Cult Product Management**

"Cargo cult" refers to a fascinating historical phenomenon where isolated Pacific Island communities, after observing WWII military airbases bring cargo via airplane, built elaborate imitation runways and control towers, hoping to attract similar cargo—faithfully copying the surface-level rituals they'd observed without genuinely understanding the underlying mechanism that actually produced the original result.

**Cargo cult Product Management looks remarkably similar:** Copying surface-level practices from successful companies (Spotify's specific team structure, Amazon's specific "working backwards" PRFAQ process, Google's specific OKR cadence) without genuinely understanding the underlying context, culture, and problems those specific practices were actually designed to solve—then being genuinely confused when copying the ritual doesn't produce the same results.

**Common examples of Product Management cargo culting:**

**Adopting OKRs because "Google does OKRs"** without genuinely understanding the underlying discipline of outcome-focused goal-setting that OKRs are meant to instill, resulting in a bureaucratic quarterly ritual of writing objectives and key results that nobody genuinely references or uses to drive actual decision-making between the writing and reviewing ceremonies.

**Copying a competitor's specific feature** because it seems to be working for them, without understanding whether it's actually driving their success (versus being incidental to their success, or even actively working against their broader strategy in ways not visible from the outside) or whether your genuinely different user base and context would respond similarly.

**Implementing "Spotify's Squad Model"** organizational structure because it sounds sophisticated and successful, without understanding that even Spotify itself has significantly evolved away from the original, widely-cited version of this model, and that it was deeply specific to their particular scaling challenges and culture at a specific point in time—not a universal best practice meant for direct, unexamined replication.

**The fix:** Before adopting any practice, framework, or structure because "successful company X does this," genuinely investigate: What specific problem was this designed to solve for them? Do we actually have that same problem, in a genuinely comparable context? What was the underlying principle, not just the surface-level ritual, and how might that underlying principle actually apply (or not apply) to our specific situation?

## **Useful Mental Models Beyond Pertama, Principles**

### **Occam's Razor**

Among competing explanations or solutions, the simplest one (requiring the fewest assumptions) is often correct or preferable, all else being equal.

**Practical application:** When debugging why a feature isn't performing as expected, resist immediately jumping to complex, exotic explanations before ruling out simpler ones. "Users aren't finding this feature because the discovery entry point is poorly placed" is a simpler, more likely explanation than "users fundamentally Jangan want this type of functionality"—check the simple explanation Pertama, before concluding something more dramatic.

### **Inversion**

Daripada asking "how do we achieve success?", ask "what would guarantee failure, and how do we systematically avoid those specific things?" Sometimes it's genuinely easier to identify and avoid clear failure modes than to directly optimize for success in the abstract.

**Practical application:** Daripada asking "how do we build a great onboarding experience?", ask "what would definitely make onboarding terrible?" (Too many steps, unclear value proposition, requiring information before demonstrating value, confusing navigation). Then explicitly ensure your design avoids each identified failure mode.

### **Kedua,-Order Thinking**

Pertama,-order thinking considers only the immediate, direct effect of a decision. Kedua,-order thinking considers the subsequent effects that Pertama,-order effect will itself produce—the "and then what happens?" chain of consequences.

**Practical application:** A Pertama,-order thought might be "adding gamification badges will increase engagement" (plausible, often true in the immediate term). Kedua,-order thinking asks: "And then what happens? Will users start optimizing for badge-collection behavior rather than genuine product value, potentially degrading the actual experience for other users, or creating perverse incentives that undermine our core value proposition over time?" Many well-intentioned product decisions that seemed obviously beneficial at the Pertama,-order level have produced genuinely harmful Kedua,-order consequences that weren't adequately considered in advance.

### **Chesterton's Fence**

Before removing or changing something that currently exists (a "fence" in the metaphor), Pertama, genuinely understand why it was put there in the Pertama, place—even if the original reasoning isn't immediately obvious to you.

**Practical application:** This is the crucial counterbalance to blindly challenging every existing convention. Not every "that's just how it's always been done" practice is genuinely arbitrary or outdated—sometimes there's real, hard-won reasoning behind an existing practice that isn't immediately visible, and removing it carelessly (without Pertama, understanding the original "why") can reintroduce problems that were already solved, just not documented clearly. Pertama,-principles thinking should include genuine investigation of _why_ current practice exists before assuming it's simply inertia-driven cruft worth discarding.

## **Balancing Skepticism with Genuine Openness**

Here's an important caveat to this entire chapter: critical thinking and Pertama,-principles reasoning shouldn't curdle into reflexive, corrosive skepticism toward everything and everyone, or an exhausting need to personally re-derive every single decision from absolute scratch.

**Healthy critical thinking:**

- Selectively applies deep scrutiny to genuinely high-stakes or clearly outdated decisions
- Remains genuinely open to being convinced that existing convention is actually correct, once properly investigated (not committed to finding fault regardless of what investigation reveals)
- Respects that other genuinely thoughtful people may have already done this reasoning work, and doesn't insist on personally re-deriving everything as if no prior thought had occurred

**Unhealthy pseudo-critical-thinking:**

- Reflexively questioning everything as a habitual, performative stance (rather than genuine, selective scrutiny), which exhausts colleagues and slows decision-making without proportional benefit
- Using "have we considered Pertama, principles here?" as a rhetorical tool to dismiss others' genuine expertise or previously-done analysis, rather than as authentic inquiry
- Never actually reaching decisions because every conclusion immediately gets subjected to further, potentially infinite regress of questioning

**The balance:** Apply genuine, selective critical thinking where it matters most (high-stakes decisions, situations where context has meaningfully changed, moments where you notice unexamined "that's just how we do it" reasoning), while trusting established convention and others' prior work in lower-stakes, well-understood situations where genuine Pertama,-principles re-derivation would waste time without proportional benefit.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Unexamined inheritance of "how things are done" is one of the most common, invisible ways mediocre product decisions calcify into permanent, unquestioned fixtures—Pertama,-principles thinking (breaking problems down to fundamental truths rather than reasoning purely by analogy to existing convention) is a genuinely powerful, learnable antidote.

Reasoning by analogy is fast and often useful for lower-stakes decisions, but becomes dangerous when context has meaningfully changed or when pursuing genuinely differentiated results. Explicitly map out and interrogate embedded assumptions in significant decisions, and avoid cargo-cult Product Management—copying surface-level practices from successful companies without genuinely understanding the underlying problem those practices were designed to solve.

Useful mental models (Occam's Razor, Inversion, Kedua,-Order Thinking, Chesterton's Fence) provide practical tools for sharper reasoning—though Chesterton's Fence importantly reminds us to genuinely understand existing practice before assuming it's simply outdated cruft worth discarding.

Balance genuine critical thinking with authentic openness—selective, high-value scrutiny rather than reflexive, exhausting skepticism toward everything and everyone.
