### **The $150K LinkedIn Post That Ruined Everything**

Let me tell you about Sarah.

Sarah was crushing it as a software engineer at a solid tech company. Great salary, clear career path, respected by her team. Then one day, she saw _that_ LinkedIn post—you know the one. Some PM influencer talking about how they "Jangan code anymore but make $200K doing strategy" and "set vision without getting stuck in the details." The post had 47,000 likes and comments like "PM is the dream job!" and "Terakhir, escaped the code mines!"

Six months later, Sarah had successfully transitioned into a PM role.

Twelve months later, she was back to engineering.

"Nobody told me," she said over our exit interview coffee, "that I'd spend 60% of my time in meetings I can't control, 30% managing people's feelings, and 10% actually thinking about product. I just wanted to solve problems. Now I _am_ the problem—at least that's what the Slack threads suggest."

Sarah isn't alone. According to my very scientific poll (aka conversations with hundreds of PMs over whiskey), about 40% of people who transition into Product Management either switch back or burn out within two years.

Here's the truth bomb nobody wants to say out loud: **Product Management is a fantastic career for the right person, and an absolute nightmare for everyone else.**

This chapter isn't here to scare you away. It's here to save you from making a $50K mistake and two years of your life you'll never get back.

### **The Brutal Myth-Busting Section (AKA The Part Your PM Friends Won't Tell You)**

#### **Myth #1: "Kamu nggak perlu be technical!"**

**Reality Check:** Kamu nggak perlu _code_, but if Kamu bisa't read a technical spec, understand API limitations, or grasp why "just make it faster" isn't helpful feedback, you're going to be that PM everyone talks about in anonymous company Slack channels.

I've seen non-technical PMs succeed, but they all had one thing in common—they were intellectually curious enough to learn. If you think "technical debt" is what happens when engineers borrow your laptop, we need to talk.

#### **Myth #2: "You get to make all the decisions!"**

**Reality Check:** You make recommendations. Other people make decisions. Sometimes those people are your CEO who saw a feature on Twitter and now it's P0. Sometimes it's the algorithm you Jangan control. Sometimes it's the budget you just found out was cut by 40%.

You have influence without authority, which is a fancy way of saying you're constantly negotiating with people who can say "no" but Kamu bisa't.

#### **Myth #3: "It's less stressful than engineering!"**

**Reality Check:** Ha. Haha. HAHAHAHA.

Engineers get stressed about code. PMs get stressed about code they can't write, deadlines they can't control, stakeholders they can't fire, users who contradict each other, metrics that Jangan make sense, strategies that change every quarter, and the nagging feeling that everyone thinks they could do your job better.

Oh, and when the product fails? That's on you. When it succeeds? "Great work, team!"

#### **Myth #4: "You'll have work-life balance!"**

**Reality Check:** You're in a global role. Europe needs an answer now (it's their EOD). Asia needs a decision before their standup (it's 2 AM your time). The US needs you in three meetings that could've been emails.

Your job is to be available. Your Slack is green more than it should be. You check email on vacation because "just in case." You're basically on-call for opinions.

#### **Myth #5: "Product Management is the natural career progression from engineering/design!"**

**Reality Check:** Being good at building things doesn't mean you'll be good at deciding what to build. These are completely different skill sets.

I've seen brilliant engineers become miserable PMs because they missed the craft. I've seen talented designers hate PM life because everything became a compromise. The progression isn't vertical, it's lateral. Sometimes it's backwards.

### **So... Who Actually Thrives in Product Management?**

After watching hundreds of PMs succeed and fail, here's what I've noticed about the ones who genuinely love this job:

**The Translators:** You get dopamine from explaining complex technical concepts to sales and business goals to engineers. You're fluent in multiple "languages" and enjoy being the bridge.

**The Chaos Surfers:** Ambiguity doesn't scare you—it energizes you. Kamu bisa hold contradictory information in your head and still make decisions. When plans change (and they always do), you adapt rather than break.

**The Influence Junkies:** You're more excited about convincing someone to build your idea than building it yourself. Persuasion is your craft. You collect allies like Pokémon cards.

**The Systems Thinkers:** You see the big picture and how pieces fit together. You're that person who makes the connection everyone else missed. You think in frameworks, not just features.

**The Resilient Optimists:** Kamu bisa hear "no" seventeen times and still show up for meeting eighteen with enthusiasm. Rejection rolls off your back. You have a short memory for disappointments and a long memory for lessons.

**The Comfortably Uncomfortable:** You're okay not being the expert in the room. Kamu nggak perlu be the smartest person; you want to be the person who asks the smartest questions.

**The Energy Givers:** People leave conversations with you feeling energized, not drained. You're not a charisma machine, but you genuinely care about helping others succeed.

### **Red Flags: When PM Might Not Be Your Path**

Look, I'm going to save you a lot of pain here. If any of these deeply resonate with you, seriously reconsider:

**You need clear hierarchies and reporting structures.** PM is ambiguous. Your "team" doesn't report to you. Your authority is earned daily, not granted by title. If you need org chart clarity to feel secure, you're going to be stressed constantly.

**You hate politics.** Not Office-style drama, but the basic human reality that different people want different things and you're the one navigating it. If you just want to "focus on the work" without considering stakeholder dynamics, you're going to have a bad time.

**You need immediate visible results.** Engineering builds something and sees it work. Design creates something and sees it ship. PM plants seeds that might grow in six months. Or never. And that's normal. If you need regular wins to stay motivated, the PM cycle might crush your soul.

**You're conflict-averse.** You will disagree with people. Daily. Sometimes you'll have to say "no" to your CEO. Sometimes you'll have to push back on engineering. Sometimes you'll have to tell sales they're wrong. If confrontation makes you physically ill, this isn't it.

**Kamu perlu be liked all the time.** PMs make trade-off decisions. Every trade-off has a loser. You will disappoint people. Regularly. Someone will always be mad at you. If you need universal approval, you'll make terrible decisions trying to please everyone.

**You want deep expertise in one craft.** PM is broad, not deep. You'll know a bit about a lot. If you're someone who wants to be the _best_ at one thing, stay in your craft. PM mastery is about orchestration, not execution.

**You struggle with ambiguity.** Requirements will be vague. Data will be incomplete. Deadlines will change. Strategies will pivot. If you need everything defined before Kamu bisa move forward, you'll be paralyzed constantly.

### **The Opportunity Cost Nobody Talks About**

Here's what you're trading when you become a PM:

**You're trading craft for coordination.** You stop making things with your hands and start making things happen through other people. Some people love this. Some people feel empty.

**You're trading depth for breadth.** You'll know less and less about more and more until you know nothing about everything (hello, imposter syndrome!).

**You're trading clear metrics for fuzzy ones.** Engineers have code coverage and deployment frequency. Designers have usability scores. PMs have... "impact"? Whatever that means this quarter.

**You're trading individual contribution for team success.** Your wins are collective. Your failures feel personal. This imbalance is real.

**You're trading predictability for chaos.** Engineering has sprints. Design has processes. PM has "whatever fire is burning hottest right now."

And here's the big one nobody says out loud: **You might be trading career velocity for career ceiling.**

In engineering, the career path is clear. Junior → Mid → Senior → Staff → Principal. Each level is well-defined. The skills compound.

In PM, the jump from Senior PM to Director is murky. Not everyone makes it. Not every company even has a clear PM ladder. You might plateau earlier than you would have in your original craft.

### **Real Talk: When I Almost Quit PM**

Year four of my PM career, I hit a wall.

We'd spent six months building a feature based on "extensive user research" (read: 12 interviews and a prayer). Launch day came. Crickets. Absolute crickets. Usage was so low our analytics thought it was a bug.

The CEO called a post-mortem. Engineering was frustrated they'd wasted half a year. Design felt their work was for nothing. Sales said "I could've told you this wouldn't work" (they definitely didn't). And there I was, the PM, the one person who was supposed to _know_.

I went home that night and updated my LinkedIn. Seriously considered going back to consulting. At least there, failure was billable.

But here's what kept me in PM: the next morning, I was already thinking about _why_ it failed. What did we miss? What could we salvage? How could we pivot? My brain didn't shut off—it shifted into problem-solving mode.

That's when I realized: I didn't love the wins (though they're nice). I loved the puzzle. The complexity. The messiness of human behavior and business constraints and technical reality all colliding. I loved being the person trying to make sense of chaos.

If that story makes you think "that sounds awful, I never want to experience that"—you're right to hesitate.

If that story makes you think "but what did you learn from the failure?"—welcome to Product Management. You're going to fit right in. Or burn out spectacularly. We'll find out together!

### **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Product Management isn't a promotion—it's a career change. You're not escaping your previous job; you're trading one set of problems for a completely different (and arguably messier) set.

The role is perfect if you thrive on influence, ambiguity, and orchestration. It's terrible if you need craft, clarity, and control.

There's no shame in realizing PM isn't for you. There's only shame in being a mediocre PM who makes everyone else's life harder because you're in the wrong role.
