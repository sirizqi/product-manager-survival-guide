## **The PRD That Nobody Had Time to Read**

I once watched a PM—recently hired away from a well-known tech giant, genuinely talented, with an impressive resume—join a 12-person, pre-Series A startup and immediately start implementing the exact processes she'd used at her previous company. Comprehensive PRDs. Formal sprint ceremonies. A dedicated Slack channel for "roadmap discussion" separate from general team chat. A quarterly OKR-setting ritual complete with a shared tracking spreadsheet.

Within six weeks, the CEO pulled her aside for a difficult conversation. Not because her work was bad—the PRDs were genuinely excellent, thorough, well-reasoned. The problem was that writing a comprehensive 8-page PRD took her three days, during which the tiny engineering team (all four of them) sat partially idle waiting for direction, while a competitor shipped a comparable feature in the time it took her to finish documenting the requirements.

She'd optimized for the kind of process rigor and documentation thoroughness that genuinely serves large organizations coordinating hundreds of people across many teams. At a 12-person startup where she could literally turn her chair around and talk to every engineer directly, that same rigor was actively counterproductive—burning precious runway-limited time on coordination overhead that a tiny, co-located team simply didn't need.

She eventually adapted (she was talented, after all), but this scenario repeats constantly: **experienced PMs, trained in big-company discipline, join startups and inadvertently import an entire operating system that's fundamentally mismatched to the actual context they're now working in.** Let's make sure this doesn't happen to you.

---

## **The Defining Characteristic of Pre-PMF Startup Life: Everything Is Provisional**

Here's the single most important mindset shift required for effective pre-PMF startup PM work: **nothing is settled yet, and treating anything as settled (your target user, your core value proposition, your business model, even your product category) is a genuine strategic error, not a sign of thoroughness.**

This is uncomfortable for PMs trained in environments where foundational assumptions (who are our users, what's our core value prop) are genuinely stable and well-established, and the PM's job is optimizing and executing within that stable foundation. Pre-PMF startup life inverts this entirely: your job is _discovering_ that foundation, treating everything—including your own prior conclusions from last month—as a hypothesis awaiting further validation or invalidation.

---

## **Wearing Multiple Hats: The Reality Beyond the Cliché**

"Wear many hats" has become such a startup cliché that it's easy to forget the genuine, practical reality it describes.

**What this actually looks like day-to-day:**

You might spend Monday morning doing genuine product discovery work (user interviews, data analysis), Monday afternoon personally responding to customer support tickets because there's no dedicated support function yet, Tuesday jumping on a sales call because the founder needs product expertise to answer a prospect's technical questions, Wednesday actually testing the product yourself because there's no dedicated QA function, and Thursday helping update the pitch deck for an upcoming investor meeting because everyone pitches in on fundraising materials when needed.

**Why this is genuinely valuable, not just chaotic:** This breadth of exposure—directly touching sales, support, and even fundraising, not just abstract "product management"—builds an unusually holistic understanding of the entire business that's genuinely harder to develop in larger organizations where these functions are more siloed. Many of the best startup PMs (and eventually, startup founders) developed crucial business intuition precisely through this kind of broad, hands-on exposure.

**Why this is also genuinely exhausting, and worth acknowledging honestly:** The constant context-switching, the lack of deep focus time, and the sheer volume of "everything nobody else has time for" landing on your plate specifically because you're perceived as generally capable, creates genuine burnout risk if not managed with some intentionality (more on this in Chapter 35).

---

## **Speed vs Quality: A Genuinely Different Calculus**

In larger, more established organizations, quality bars tend to be relatively fixed and non-negotiable—a certain level of polish, edge-case handling, and testing rigor is simply expected, regardless of competitive pressure or timeline urgency.

**Pre-PMF startups operate under a fundamentally different calculus:** Speed to learning often matters more than polish, because the actual value of any given feature is still genuinely unknown and unvalidated. Spending three weeks perfecting a feature that turns out nobody wants is a far worse outcome than shipping something rougher in three days that quickly reveals genuine user response, even if that response is "nobody cares about this at all."

**A useful mental model: "Minimum Viable" genuinely means minimum.** Not minimum-but-still-professionally-polished. Minimum sufficient to generate genuine learning about whether an underlying hypothesis is correct. This often means shipping things that would be genuinely embarrassing by larger-company standards—rough edges, limited functionality, even manual processes standing in for what would eventually become automated features (the classic "concierge MVP" approach, where you manually deliver a service that will eventually be productized, specifically to validate demand before investing in automation).

**The genuine risk on the other side:** Some startups swing too far toward "move fast and break things" without appropriate discernment about which quality corners are genuinely acceptable to cut (rough UI polish on an early feature) versus which corners create genuine risk (basic security vulnerabilities, data loss risks, or core functionality that's so broken it prevents genuine learning about the underlying hypothesis you're actually trying to test).

---

## **Resource Constraint Navigation: Doing More With Genuinely Less**

Beyond just "wearing multiple hats" personally, pre-PMF startup PM work requires genuine skill in extracting maximum learning and progress from severely limited resources across the board—limited engineering capacity, limited design resources, limited (or no) dedicated research budget, and critically, limited runway (time before the company runs out of money).

**Practical resource-constrained discovery techniques:**

**Concierge MVPs:** Manually deliver the value proposition before building any actual product infrastructure, testing genuine demand and understanding user needs deeply before investing scarce engineering time in automation.

**Wizard of Oz testing:** Present users with what appears to be a functioning automated product, while manually performing the "automated" work behind the scenes—genuinely validating whether the value proposition resonates before investing in actual technical build-out.

**Landing page tests:** Before building anything, create a landing page describing the proposed product/feature, driving genuine traffic (even small amounts) to gauge actual interest through sign-ups or purchase intent, before committing scarce development resources.

**Leveraging no-code/low-code tools aggressively** for early validation, rather than defaulting to full custom development for hypotheses that haven't yet been validated as worth that investment.

**The critical discipline this requires:** Resisting the instinct (common among PMs trained in larger organizations with more resources) to default toward "properly built" solutions when a scrappier, faster validation approach would generate equivalent or even better learning at a fraction of the resource cost.

## **The Founder-PM Dynamic: A Genuinely Unique Relationship**

If you're a PM at an early-stage startup, your relationship with the founder(s) deserves specific, dedicated attention, because it functions quite differently than typical PM-executive relationships at larger, more established companies.

**Why this relationship is genuinely different:** Founders typically have deep, often deeply personal conviction about the product vision—this is usually their idea, their bet, sometimes their life savings and years of personal sacrifice invested in a specific vision. This creates both genuine opportunity (founders often have crucial market insight and conviction that shouldn't be casually dismissed) and genuine risk (founders can be resistant to disconfirming evidence specifically because of this deep personal investment, in ways that a professional executive at a larger company, with less personal identity fusion with the specific product, typically isn't).

**Practical navigation:**

**Respect genuine founder insight and market conviction**, especially regarding customer understanding and market timing—founders often have earned, hard-won intuition from years of thinking obsessively about this specific problem space, even if they can't always articulate that intuition in the structured frameworks you might be more comfortable with.

**Bring rigorous discovery and data as a genuine complement to founder intuition, not as an adversarial challenge to their authority.** Framing data-driven pushback as "let's validate this together" rather than "I think you're wrong" respects the genuine emotional and professional stakes founders have in their vision, while still bringing appropriate rigor to decision-making.

**Recognize when founder conviction is genuinely valuable signal versus when it's becoming an obstacle to necessary pivoting.** This is genuinely difficult to discern in the moment, and there's no universal formula—but the willingness to have honest, sometimes uncomfortable conversations about this distinction, rather than either blind deference or dismissive data-worship, is a crucial skill specific to this particular working relationship.

## **The Classic Mistake: Bringing Big Company Process to a Startup**

We opened this chapter with a specific story of this exact mistake, but it's worth explicitly cataloging the most common manifestations:

**Excessive documentation rigor** relative to team size and communication needs—when you can literally talk to every stakeholder directly and immediately, extensive written documentation designed for asynchronous, cross-team coordination at scale becomes overhead rather than genuine value.

**Formal ceremony overhead** (extensive sprint planning, detailed retrospectives, formal stakeholder review cycles) that assumes a scale and complexity of coordination that a small, tightly-coordinated team simply doesn't yet require.

**Premature specialization and process division** (separate discovery and delivery tracks, formal handoff processes between research and design and engineering) that adds coordination overhead without corresponding benefit when the same few people are doing all of these functions anyway, often simultaneously.

**Excessive caution and risk-aversion** inherited from larger-company norms where mistakes are more costly (due to scale and reputation) and less easily reversed, when startup context often genuinely favors faster, more experimental, higher-risk-tolerance decision-making given the different cost-benefit calculus of an organization still searching for its fundamental value proposition.

**The genuine calibration needed:** This doesn't mean _zero_ process or documentation is appropriate at every startup—some minimal structure genuinely helps even small teams avoid chaos and miscommunication. The skill is calibrating process investment specifically to your actual team size, coordination needs, and stage, rather than defaulting to whatever process intensity you're personally accustomed to from prior experience at different-stage companies.

---

## **What Genuinely Matters More at Pre-PMF Startups**

Given everything discussed above, here's what should actually receive your primary focus and energy as a PM in this specific context:

**Ruthless focus on learning velocity**—how quickly can you validate or invalidate your riskiest assumptions, not how polished or comprehensive your process looks.

**Direct, frequent, low-overhead communication** rather than formal documentation and process—a quick Slack message or hallway conversation often serves better than a comprehensive written spec, given team size and coordination needs.

**Genuine comfort with ambiguity and rapid direction changes**, since pivoting based on new learning isn't a failure of planning—it's the entire point of this stage, and resistance to pivoting when genuine evidence warrants it is a more significant risk than "wasting" prior effort on a direction that's now being revised.

**Extreme resourcefulness**, finding scrappy, low-cost ways to generate genuine learning and progress rather than defaulting to "properly resourced" approaches that assume a level of budget and team size you don't actually have.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Pre-PMF startup PM work requires treating everything—target users, value proposition, business model—as provisional hypotheses awaiting validation, rather than settled foundations to optimize within, fundamentally inverting the mindset appropriate for more established organizational contexts.

Embrace genuine multiple-hat-wearing as valuable holistic business exposure (while managing the genuine burnout risk this creates), and recalibrate your speed-versus-quality calculus toward learning velocity over polish, using resource-constrained validation techniques (concierge MVPs, Wizard of Oz testing, landing page tests) rather than defaulting to fully-built solutions for unvalidated hypotheses.

Navigate the founder-PM relationship with genuine respect for founder market conviction, while still bringing rigorous discovery as a complement rather than an adversarial challenge—and develop the difficult judgment to distinguish valuable founder intuition from unhealthy resistance to necessary pivoting.

Above all, resist importing big-company process rigor (extensive documentation, formal ceremonies, premature specialization) that serves large-scale coordination needs your small, tightly-coordinated team doesn't actually have yet.
