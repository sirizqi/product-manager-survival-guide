## **Why This Chapter Exists**

Throughout this book, we've explored individual mistakes within specific contexts—discovery theater in Chapter 4, overcommitment in Chapter 7, cargo cult management in Chapter 19. This chapter serves a different purpose: **synthesizing the most consequential, recurring failure patterns into a single, practical reference**, because sometimes seeing the pattern named clearly and concisely, stripped of surrounding context, creates a different kind of recognition than encountering it embedded within a longer discussion.

Consider this chapter less a new set of ideas and more a diagnostic checklist—the kind of thing worth revisiting periodically throughout your career, not just reading once and setting aside.

---

## **1. Building for Yourself, Not Users**

**What it looks like:** Prioritizing features based on your own preferences, aesthetic sensibilities, or assumptions about what "should" be valuable, rather than genuine, validated user need.

**Why it happens:** You are, after all, a human with your own genuine preferences, and it's psychologically easier to trust your own intuition than to consistently do the harder work of genuine discovery (Chapter 4).

**The fix:** Regularly ask, explicitly: "Am I building this because genuine research/data supports it, or because it appeals to my own personal taste?" (Revisit Chapter 18's distinction between genuine product sense and personal preference.)

---

## **2. Feature Factory Syndrome**

**What it looks like:** Measuring success by shipping volume (number of features released) rather than genuine impact or outcome achieved.

**Why it happens:** Shipping volume is easy to measure and visibly demonstrate; genuine impact often requires more patience, more sophisticated measurement, and acceptance that not everything shippable is actually valuable.

**The fix:** Explicitly track and report on outcome metrics, not just shipping velocity (Chapter 8's distinction between vanity and actionable metrics), and resist organizational pressure to demonstrate "productivity" through pure output volume.

---

## **3. Ignoring Technical Debt**

**What it looks like:** Consistently deprioritizing technical debt paydown in favor of visible, exciting feature work, until accumulated debt eventually creates crisis-level velocity or reliability problems.

**Why it happens:** Technical debt is invisible to most stakeholders and doesn't generate the same visible excitement as new features (Chapter 6, Chapter 14).

**The fix:** Advocate for protected, systematic technical debt capacity rather than letting it compete ad-hoc against every shiny feature request in a popularity contest it will predictably lose.

---

## **4. Over-Promising Timelines**

**What it looks like:** Committing to unrealistic deadlines under pressure, then either missing them repeatedly or forcing unsustainable crunch to hit arbitrary dates.

**Why it happens:** Optimism bias, political pressure to promise more than genuinely feasible, and poor estimation practices that treat engineering estimates as fixed commitments rather than probabilistic ranges (Chapter 7).

**The fix:** Build in genuine buffer capacity, treat estimates as ranges rather than points, and practice saying no during planning rather than quietly failing later.

---

## **5. Copying Competitors Blindly**

**What it looks like:** Building features primarily because a competitor has them, without genuine validation that your specific users need the same thing, or understanding why that feature actually works (or doesn't) for the competitor's specific context.

**Why it happens:** Competitive feature parity feels safer and more defensible than genuine, independent strategic reasoning, particularly under stakeholder pressure (Chapter 5's competitive analysis discussion, Chapter 19's cargo cult warning).

**The fix:** Understand the underlying job/need a competitor's feature addresses, and validate whether your specific users genuinely share that need, rather than assuming feature parity is inherently strategically necessary.

---

## **6. Ignoring Data Signals**

**What it looks like:** Continuing to invest in a direction despite genuine, accumulating data suggesting it isn't working, due to sunk cost attachment or unwillingness to acknowledge being wrong.

**Why it happens:** Genuine psychological difficulty in updating strongly-held beliefs, particularly for initiatives you've personally championed (Chapter 20's decision-making discussion, Chapter 37's failure-processing discussion).

**The fix:** Build explicit, pre-committed checkpoints for evaluating initiatives against genuine data, ideally established before you have emotional investment in a specific outcome, making course-correction less personally threatening when data warrants it.

---

## **7. Poor Prioritization (Political, Not Rigorous)**

**What it looks like:** Prioritization driven primarily by whoever's loudest, most senior, or most persistent, rather than genuine strategic reasoning and rigorous evaluation (Chapter 6).

**The fix:** Make prioritization reasoning explicit and visible, use consistent frameworks even when imperfect, and have the organizational courage to push back on purely political prioritization pressure when genuinely warranted.

---

## **8. Communication Breakdown**

**What it looks like:** Burying key messages in excessive detail, failing to adapt communication for different audiences, or maintaining silence during difficulty rather than proactive transparency (Chapter 11).

**The fix:** Lead with conclusions, tailor depth and language to specific audiences, and practice proactive communication about problems rather than hoping issues resolve before anyone notices.

---

## **9. Burning Bridges**

**What it looks like:** Treating cross-functional relationships transactionally, taking credit unfairly, or damaging trust through inconsistency or broken commitments (Chapter 13, Chapter 14, Chapter 15).

**The fix:** Consistently invest in genuine relationship "deposits" before you need to make significant "withdrawals," and treat trust-building as an ongoing practice, not a one-time achievement.

---

## **10. Not Saying "No" Enough**

**What it looks like:** Attempting to satisfy every stakeholder request, resulting in diluted focus, overcommitment, and ultimately worse outcomes for everyone than genuine, disciplined prioritization would have produced (Chapter 6).

**The fix:** Develop genuine comfort with disappointing people gracefully, backed by clear reasoning connected to strategy—recognizing that trying to please everyone typically produces worse outcomes than honest, well-reasoned trade-offs.

---

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

These ten patterns—building for yourself rather than users, feature factory syndrome, ignoring technical debt, over-promising timelines, blindly copying competitors, ignoring data signals, political rather than rigorous prioritization, communication breakdown, burning bridges, and insufficient willingness to say no—represent the most consequential, recurring PM failure modes explored throughout this book.

None of these mistakes are exotic or require rare circumstances to manifest; they emerge from genuinely common psychological and organizational pressures that every PM will encounter repeatedly throughout their career. Recognizing these patterns explicitly, and periodically revisiting this list as a genuine diagnostic check on your own current practice, offers more practical value than any single reading ever could.

In our final section, we shift from diagnosis to genuine implementation—starting with your first 90 days in any new PM role, and building through the practical templates, interview preparation, and personal brand-building that translate this book's frameworks into genuine, sustained career practice.
