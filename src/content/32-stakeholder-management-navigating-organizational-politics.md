## **The PM Who Refused to Play the Game (And Lost Anyway)**

I once worked alongside a brilliant PM—let's call him Daniel—who prided himself on being "above the politics." He'd say things like "I just focus on the data and let the work speak for itself" and "I'm not here to play games, I'm here to build good products."

Admirable in theory. Here's what happened in practice: Daniel consistently built genuinely excellent features backed by rigorous research. He also consistently got deprioritized in resourcing conversations, excluded from key strategic discussions he should've been part of, and eventually passed over for a promotion he was technically more qualified for than the person who got it.

Why? Because while Daniel was heads-down doing "the real work," his less technically rigorous but more politically aware peer was building relationships with key decision-makers, understanding what different stakeholders actually cared about, and making sure his contributions were visible to the people who controlled resourcing and promotion decisions.

Daniel wasn't naive because he lacked skill. He was naive because he believed "politics" was optional—something beneath serious, competent people who let their work speak for itself. Here's the uncomfortable truth: **politics isn't a corrupt deviation from how organizations should work. Politics is simply what happens whenever multiple humans with different interests, incentives, and perspectives need to coordinate toward shared (or sometimes conflicting) goals.** Refusing to engage with this reality doesn't make you above it—it just makes you unusually vulnerable to it.

Let's talk about stakeholder management as it actually exists, not as we wish it worked.

---

## **Mapping Stakeholder Influence and Interest**

Before you can navigate stakeholder dynamics effectively, you need genuine clarity on who actually matters and why.

**The classic 2x2 framework:**

**High Influence, High Interest (Manage Closely):** These people can significantly impact your work and are actively engaged with it. Your direct manager, key engineering leads, executives directly overseeing your product area. Invest heavily in these relationships—regular updates, genuine collaboration, addressing concerns proactively.

**High Influence, Low Interest (Keep Satisfied):** These people could derail your work if unhappy, but aren't naturally paying close attention day-to-day. Often senior executives outside your direct area, or leaders of adjacent teams. Keep them appropriately informed without overwhelming them—brief, relevant updates that respect their limited bandwidth for your specific work.

**Low Influence, High Interest (Keep Informed):** These people care deeply but don't have significant power to change direction. Junior team members, enthusiastic individual contributors on adjacent teams. Keep them engaged and informed—they can become valuable allies and advocates, even without formal authority.

**Low Influence, Low Interest (Monitor):** Minimal investment needed here, though don't ignore entirely—circumstances can shift someone's influence or interest level over time.

**Why this mapping matters practically:** Most PMs instinctively spend equal time across all stakeholders, or worse, spend disproportionate time on whoever's most vocal (regardless of actual influence). Deliberately mapping this out helps you allocate your limited relationship-building time and communication energy where it actually matters most.

## **Managing Up, Down, and Sideways**

### **Managing Up (Your Manager, Executives)**

**What this actually requires:** Understanding what your manager and their manager actually care about and are measured on, then framing your work in terms that connect to those priorities—not just what you personally find interesting or important.

**Common mistake:** Assuming your manager has full context on everything you're doing. They don't. Proactively surface both wins and problems, rather than assuming visibility happens automatically. Silence gets interpreted as either "everything's fine" or "this person isn't communicating," neither of which serves you well if something's actually going wrong.

**A particularly important skill:** Managing your manager's own stakeholder pressures. Your manager is themselves navigating pressure from their manager, other departments, and the board. Understanding this context helps you interpret seemingly arbitrary priority shifts (they're often not arbitrary from your manager's vantage point, even if they feel that way from yours).

### **Managing Down (If You Have Direct Reports or Junior PMs)**

**What this actually requires:** Providing genuine context and reasoning behind decisions, not just directives. People execute far better and with more genuine buy-in when they understand the "why," not just the "what."

**Common mistake:** Shielding junior team members from organizational complexity and politics "to protect them," which actually leaves them unprepared and confused when they inevitably encounter that same complexity directly, without having developed any skills to navigate it.

### **Managing Sideways (Peer PMs, Other Department Leads)**

**What this actually requires:** Building genuine collaborative relationships before you need something from them, not just transactionally reaching out only when you need a favor or resource.

**Common mistake:** Treating peer relationships purely competitively (especially common among PMs competing for the same promotion or scarce engineering resources), which poisons collaboration and information-sharing that would benefit everyone, including yourself, in the long run.

## **Dealing with the HiPPO (Highest Paid Person's Opinion)**

We touched on this in Chapter 6, but it deserves deeper treatment here because it's fundamentally a stakeholder management challenge, not just a prioritization framework challenge.

**Why HiPPOs are genuinely tricky:** Senior executives often do have valuable pattern recognition from experience—their instincts aren't always wrong just because they lack your specific day-to-day data. The challenge isn't dismissing their input; it's ensuring decisions are made with appropriate rigor regardless of who's proposing them, and that seniority alone doesn't automatically override careful analysis when the two genuinely conflict.

**A practical approach:**

1. **Take the input seriously, immediately.** Don't dismiss or visibly roll your eyes, even internally. This destroys trust fast and makes future collaboration harder.
    
2. **Ask clarifying questions that surface their underlying reasoning.** "What's driving this? Have you seen something specific that suggests this direction?" Sometimes you'll discover valuable context you didn't have. Sometimes you'll discover it's based on a single anecdote or a competitor's Twitter announcement.
    
3. **Bring data and reasoning, not opinions, in response.** "Here's what we're seeing in our research/data that's relevant to this" lands very differently than "I don't think that's a good idea," even if you're ultimately advocating for a different direction.
    
4. **Know when to hold your ground and when to yield gracefully.** Not every battle is worth fighting. Reserve your strongest pushback for genuinely high-stakes decisions, and pick your battles rather than contesting every single directive that doesn't match your initial instinct.

## **Navigating Conflicting Stakeholder Demands**

This is the defining daily reality of PM work: Sales wants Feature A to close a big deal. Engineering wants to prioritize technical debt that's slowing everyone down. The CEO wants to explore a new market segment. Customer Success is reporting a specific pain point causing churn. All of this, simultaneously, with finite resources.

**The instinct to avoid:** Trying to quietly satisfy everyone a little bit, which usually means satisfying nobody meaningfully and diluting your actual impact across too many half-finished initiatives.

**A healthier approach:**

**1. Make the conflict visible, not hidden.** Rather than making a unilateral decision behind closed doors and hoping nobody notices the trade-off, explicitly surface the competing demands to relevant stakeholders: "We have four significant asks this quarter and capacity for roughly two. Here's how I'm thinking about prioritizing, and here's why—I want to make sure everyone understands the trade-off before we commit."

**2. Use your strategy (Chapter 5) as the tiebreaker, not personal preference or whoever asked most recently/loudly.** If your strategic priorities are genuinely clear, conflicting stakeholder requests become easier to evaluate against a consistent standard, rather than an exhausting case-by-case political negotiation every single time.

**3. Give people a genuine hearing, even when you ultimately don't prioritize their request.** Stakeholders whose requests get deprioritized are far more accepting of that outcome when they feel genuinely heard and understand the reasoning, versus feeling dismissed or ignored entirely.

**4. Follow up on deprioritized items, don't let them silently disappear forever.** "We didn't prioritize this last quarter, but I want you to know it's still on our radar—here's roughly when we might revisit it" maintains trust far better than requests disappearing into a black hole, never to be mentioned again.

## **Building Coalitions and Allies**

Here's something rarely taught explicitly: **your ability to drive change in an organization correlates strongly with how many genuine allies you've built, not just how good your ideas are.**

**Practical coalition-building tactics:**

**Pre-socialize major decisions before the "official" meeting.** If you're proposing something significant, have informal conversations with key stakeholders beforehand to surface concerns and build initial buy-in. The formal meeting should ideally confirm alignment already largely established, rather than being the first time people encounter your idea and react defensively in a public setting.

**Give credit generously and genuinely.** When someone contributes a good idea or helps you succeed, publicly acknowledge it. This isn't just kindness (though it is that)—it builds a reservoir of goodwill and reciprocal support that pays dividends when you need help or buy-in later.

**Find your genuine champions early.** Identify people—regardless of formal seniority—who believe in your work and are willing to advocate for it in rooms you're not in. Cultivate these relationships deliberately, not just opportunistically when you need something specific.

**Understand what different stakeholders actually need to feel successful**, and look for genuine win-win framing rather than purely transactional asks. If you understand that your engineering lead is evaluated on system reliability, framing your request in terms of how it also improves reliability (when genuinely true) creates authentic alignment rather than a one-sided favor.

## **The HiPPO's Cousin: Dealing with the "Squeaky Wheel"**

Beyond hierarchical power, there's another political dynamic: the persistently vocal stakeholder who gets outsized attention simply through sheer persistence and volume, regardless of the actual merit or priority of their request relative to quieter, equally important needs.

**Why this is dangerous:** If your prioritization process effectively rewards whoever complains loudest and most frequently, you're optimizing for stakeholder patience and persistence rather than genuine business or user value—a recipe for prioritization chaos and resentment among stakeholders who don't play this particular game.

**The fix:** Have a consistent, transparent process for evaluating and communicating about requests (referencing your strategic priorities), so that volume and persistence alone don't override that process. This requires genuine discipline, especially when the squeaky wheel is someone senior or intimidating.

## **When Politics Becomes Toxic (And How to Tell the Difference)**

Everything discussed so far describes _healthy_ organizational politics—navigating legitimate differences in perspective, incentive, and priority among reasonable people trying to do good work.

**Toxic politics looks different:**

- Credit-stealing and blame-shifting as standard operating procedure
- Information hoarding used as a power tactic rather than genuine oversight
- Undermining colleagues behind their backs rather than addressing disagreements directly
- Decisions made based purely on personal loyalty/favoritism rather than any legitimate business reasoning
- A culture where raising honest concerns or disagreements is met with retaliation rather than genuine consideration

**If you're navigating genuinely toxic politics** (not just the normal friction of competing legitimate interests), the strategies in this chapter will only get you so far. We'll dig much deeper into recognizing and navigating (or exiting) toxic organizational environments in Chapter 36.

## **Case Study: The Three-Way Stakeholder War**

Let me walk through a real (details altered) scenario that captures the complexity of this work.

Our CEO wanted to expand into enterprise clients (bigger deals, more prestige, board-pleasing growth numbers). Our CTO wanted to prioritize a significant technical infrastructure overhaul (our system genuinely couldn't scale reliably much further without it). Our Head of Sales wanted a specific set of custom features to close three mid-sized deals already in the pipeline.

All three had legitimate reasoning. All three required most of our available engineering capacity for the coming two quarters. None of them wanted to hear "we can only do one of these."

**What I did:**

**1. Got genuinely curious about the underlying "why" behind each request**, not just the surface-level ask. The CEO's enterprise push connected to a board commitment about revenue diversification. The CTO's infrastructure concern connected to actual reliability incidents that had already started affecting customers. The sales deals connected to specific revenue targets for the quarter that mattered for the broader company's runway.

**2. Brought data illustrating the actual trade-off costs of each path**, including what would likely happen if we deprioritized each option (revenue impact of delayed enterprise push, reliability risk of continued infrastructure delay, specific deal risk if custom features weren't built).

**3. Facilitated a joint conversation (not separate one-on-one negotiations)** where all three stakeholders heard each other's reasoning directly, rather than me relaying secondhand summaries that could be discounted or misunderstood.

**4. Proposed a sequenced compromise**: address the most critical, safety-related infrastructure work first (a smaller scope than the CTO's full ideal ask, but addressing the most acute reliability risk), while committing to enterprise features in the following quarter with sales deals prioritized based on specific deal size and probability of closing.

**The outcome:** Nobody got 100% of what they wanted. Everyone understood why, because they'd heard each other's reasoning directly and seen the actual trade-off data, not just experienced a unilateral decision handed down from above. This is usually the realistic best-case outcome in genuine stakeholder conflicts—not perfect satisfaction, but genuine understanding and legitimacy behind whatever trade-off gets made.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Politics isn't a corrupt deviation from "real work"—it's the inevitable reality of coordinating humans with different interests, incentives, and perspectives, and refusing to engage with this reality doesn't make you above it, just vulnerable to it.

Map stakeholder influence and interest deliberately, rather than instinctively spending equal time everywhere or disproportionate time on whoever's loudest. Manage up, down, and sideways with genuine context-sharing rather than assuming visibility happens automatically.

Handle HiPPOs by taking their input seriously while still bringing rigorous data and reasoning, rather than either blind deference or dismissive eye-rolling. Make conflicting stakeholder demands visible rather than hidden, use your strategy as a consistent tiebreaker, and build genuine coalitions and allies proactively, not just when you desperately need a favor.
