## **The Meeting That Had Forty People in It**

I remember the exact moment I realized our company had fundamentally changed. We were planning a mid-sized feature launch—nothing revolutionary, just a solid, useful addition to our core product. Eighteen months earlier, a launch like this would have involved a conversation between me, two engineers, and a designer, wrapped up in an afternoon.

This time, the planning meeting had forty people on the invite. Legal needed to review terms of service implications. A newly-hired Growth team wanted input on activation flow integration. Customer Success needed advance notice to prepare support documentation. A separate Platform team needed to confirm API compatibility. Marketing had three different sub-teams (content, paid acquisition, lifecycle) all wanting coordination touchpoints.

I sat in that meeting feeling a specific, disorienting frustration: everything that had made me effective eighteen months earlier—moving fast, deciding things quickly, talking directly to whoever needed to be involved—suddenly felt clumsy and inadequate against this new scale of coordination complexity. I hadn't gotten worse at my job. **The job itself had fundamentally changed underneath me, and I hadn't yet adapted my own operating model to match.**

This is the central challenge of growth-stage PM work: the skills, instincts, and processes that worked brilliantly at your company's earlier stage don't just become less necessary—they can become actively counterproductive if you don't consciously evolve your approach alongside your company's genuine scaling reality.

## **The Defining Characteristic of Growth Stage: Coordination Complexity Explodes**

Where pre-PMF startups optimize for learning velocity with minimal process overhead, growth-stage companies face a fundamentally different core challenge: **coordinating an increasing number of specialized people and teams, each with genuine, legitimate expertise and stake in decisions, without letting that coordination complexity grind everything to a halt.**

This isn't simply "more people doing the same things." It's genuine specialization emerging—dedicated teams for functions that used to be handled informally by whoever had bandwidth, each developing real expertise and legitimate claim to influence decisions within their domain.

---

## **Process vs Agility: Finding the Genuinely Right Balance**

Here's the specific tension every growth-stage PM navigates: too little process, and coordination breaks down catastrophically as team size exceeds what informal, ad-hoc communication can handle. Too much process, inherited reflexively from larger, more mature organizations, and you sacrifice the genuine speed and agility that got you to this stage in the first place.

**Signs you need more process than you currently have:**

- Repeated, avoidable miscommunication or duplicated work because relevant people weren't looped in on decisions affecting them
- Decisions made in one part of the organization that unexpectedly conflict with decisions made elsewhere, discovered only after both have been executed
- New team members struggling significantly to understand "how things work here" because critical knowledge exists only in individual people's heads, never documented

**Signs you have too much process for your actual current needs:**

- Simple, relatively low-stakes decisions require excessive approval chains or stakeholder sign-off relative to their genuine complexity or risk
- People spend more time updating status in tracking tools than actually doing the underlying work those tools are meant to track
- Genuine urgency gets lost in process—by the time you've completed all "required" steps, the market opportunity or competitive window has meaningfully shifted

**The practical calibration approach:** Introduce process incrementally, specifically in response to genuine, observed coordination failures—not preemptively based on what larger companies do, or what feels "more professional" and mature. If you haven't actually experienced the specific coordination problem a given process is meant to solve, you probably don't need that process yet, regardless of how standard it might be at companies further along in scaling.

---

## **Multiple Product Lines: The Coordination Challenge Nobody Warns You About**

As growth-stage companies expand, they frequently develop multiple product lines or major feature areas, each potentially owned by different PMs, creating genuine coordination challenges that didn't exist when the entire company rallied around a single, unified product.

**Common friction points:**

**Resource competition between product lines**, each with legitimate business cases for engineering investment, competing for genuinely limited shared engineering capacity—requiring more formal cross-product prioritization mechanisms than informal, single-product prioritization required.

**User experience consistency challenges**, as different product teams, moving at different speeds with different immediate priorities, can inadvertently create a fragmented, inconsistent overall product experience if not actively coordinated.

**Shared infrastructure/platform dependencies**, where one product team's technical decisions genuinely affect other teams' capabilities, requiring more deliberate coordination than existed when a single team owned the entire technical stack end-to-end.

**A practical approach:** Establish lightweight, regular cross-product-team syncs (not necessarily elaborate, just genuinely consistent) specifically focused on surfacing dependencies, shared infrastructure needs, and consistency considerations—catching coordination issues proactively rather than discovering conflicts only after each team has already built incompatible or redundant solutions independently.

## **Building the PM Function From Scratch: A Unique Growth-Stage Challenge**

Many growth-stage companies face a specific challenge: they've operated with one or two generalist PMs (or even founders directly performing PM functions) through their earlier stage, and now need to genuinely build out a proper, scaled PM function and team—often for the first time in the company's history.

**If you're the person tasked with this (often a first PM hire, or first PM leader hire, at a growth-stage company):**

**Resist immediately implementing an elaborate, fully-formed PM process framework** copied wholesale from a previous, more mature company you worked at. Instead, genuinely diagnose what specific coordination and decision-making challenges this particular company is currently experiencing, and build process incrementally to address those specific, observed needs.

**Establish foundational practices before scaling complexity:** Basic, consistent practices around how product decisions get documented and communicated, how prioritization decisions get made and explained, and how discovery/research insights get captured and shared—these foundational elements matter more initially than sophisticated frameworks that assume foundational practices are already solidly in place.

**Be genuinely thoughtful about the first PM hiring wave** (discussed more below)—these initial hires will significantly shape team culture and practice for years to come, making this hiring decision unusually high-stakes compared to hiring into an already-established, culturally-mature PM team.

---

## **The First Hiring Wave: Disproportionately High-Stakes Decisions**

If you're involved in building out a PM team at a growth-stage company, understand that your first several hires carry outsized influence on the team culture and practice that will persist long after those specific individuals may have moved on.

**Why this matters more than typical hiring:** Later PM hires will largely absorb and adapt to whatever culture and practice norms the initial hires establish. If your first few PM hires develop excellent habits (genuine discovery rigor, healthy stakeholder collaboration, appropriate process calibration), this tends to self-reinforce as new hires observe and adopt these same behaviors. If early hires develop poor habits (research theater, political maneuvering over genuine collaboration, either excessive or insufficient process), this also tends to self-reinforce in ways that become genuinely difficult to unwind later.

**Practical implications:** Invest disproportionate hiring rigor and cultural clarity in these initial hires, even if this means a slower hiring process than the immediate resourcing pressure might otherwise suggest. Getting this genuinely right matters more, at this specific inflection point, than filling seats quickly.

## **Scaling Challenges Beyond Just Headcount**

Growth-stage scaling challenges extend well beyond simply "more people, more coordination"—several specific dynamics deserve direct attention.

**Institutional knowledge fragmentation:** As the company grows beyond the size where everyone genuinely knows everything happening across the organization, critical context and history that used to be common knowledge becomes genuinely inaccessible to newer team members, unless deliberately documented and made discoverable.

**Cultural dilution risk:** The specific values and working norms that made your earlier-stage team effective can genuinely dilute as headcount grows faster than deliberate culture-transmission efforts, resulting in a larger team that superficially resembles your earlier culture in stated values, but genuinely operates quite differently in practice.

**Increasing distance between decision-makers and ground-truth reality:** As organizational layers increase, executives and senior leaders can become genuinely more distant from direct user interaction and ground-level operational reality, requiring more deliberate mechanisms (regular user research readouts, direct customer interaction programs, transparent data access) to maintain genuine ground-truth connection at senior levels.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Growth-stage PM work centers on managing exploding coordination complexity as genuine specialization emerges across the organization—requiring conscious evolution beyond the informal, low-process approaches that worked at earlier stages, without over-correcting into excessive, prematurely-mature process that sacrifices genuine agility.

Calibrate process investment incrementally, in direct response to observed coordination failures, rather than preemptively adopting larger-company norms. Navigate multiple product line coordination challenges through lightweight, consistent cross-team syncs, and if you're building a PM function from scratch, recognize that your first hiring wave carries outsized, long-lasting influence on team culture worth disproportionate hiring investment and care.

Stay vigilant against institutional knowledge fragmentation and cultural dilution as headcount scales, and deliberately maintain ground-truth connection at senior levels even as organizational distance from direct user interaction naturally increases.
