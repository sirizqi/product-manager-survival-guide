## **The Price Increase That Increased Revenue (And Decreased Customers)**

A SaaS company I advised decided to raise prices by 40% based on straightforward competitive benchmarking—they discovered they were significantly underpriced relative to comparable competitors offering similar functionality, and reasoned that customers would tolerate the increase given the genuine value being delivered.

The immediate result: monthly recurring revenue increased by roughly 15% (some customers left, but their combined lost revenue was smaller than the gain from remaining customers paying more). By pure short-term revenue metrics, this looked like an unambiguous success.

Eight months later, more troubling data emerged: customer acquisition had slowed significantly (word-of-mouth referrals, previously a major growth channel, had dropped noticeably), and support tickets citing "pricing frustration" had become a recurring theme in exit interviews with churning customers, even those who'd technically tolerated the price increase for several months before eventually leaving anyway.

**The mistake wasn't necessarily the price increase itself**—competitive benchmarking suggested genuine room for higher pricing. **The mistake was treating pricing as a purely competitive/financial calculation, disconnected from genuine psychology, customer relationship dynamics, and long-term trust considerations that don't show up cleanly in a simple "compare to competitor pricing" spreadsheet.**

This chapter explores pricing and monetization with the psychological, strategic, and ethical nuance this genuinely complex topic deserves—well beyond simplistic "charge what the market will bear" advice.

## **Pricing Psychology: Beyond Simple Economic Rationality**

Classical economic theory assumes people make pricing decisions through rational cost-benefit calculation. Genuine human psychology is considerably messier, and understanding these psychological patterns is crucial for effective pricing strategy.

**Anchoring effects:** The first price a customer encounters significantly shapes their perception of subsequent prices, even when that initial anchor is somewhat arbitrary. This is why displaying a higher-priced "premium" tier alongside a more moderately-priced option can make the moderate option feel like better relative value, even if the moderate option's absolute price hasn't changed.

**Loss aversion:** People generally feel the pain of losing something more intensely than the pleasure of gaining something equivalent. This is why "grandfather" pricing (allowing existing customers to keep current pricing when you raise prices for new customers) often generates less backlash than uniformly raising prices on everyone—existing customers experience a potential price increase as a loss, while new customers simply experience the current price as their baseline reality, without the same loss-aversion-triggered resistance.

**Charm pricing:** The well-documented tendency for prices ending in 9 (or just below round numbers) to feel meaningfully cheaper than they mathematically are ($9.99 feeling significantly cheaper than $10.00, despite the trivial actual difference), reflecting how people cognitively process the leftmost digits of a price more heavily than precise mathematical calculation would suggest.

**The paradox of choice in pricing tiers:** Offering too many pricing options can create decision paralysis and actually reduce conversion, even though it theoretically provides more precise value-matching for diverse customer needs. Many successful pricing strategies deliberately limit tier options (often to exactly three) specifically to avoid this paralysis while still providing meaningful choice.

## **Freemium vs Premium vs Tiered: Choosing the Right Model**

**Pure Premium (paid-only, no free tier):** Works well when your product delivers clear, immediate value that customers can readily understand and evaluate before purchase, and when your target customers have genuine budget authority and established purchasing processes (common in B2B contexts with clear ROI justification).

**Freemium:** Works well when network effects benefit from a large user base (more free users increase value for paying users, or free users eventually convert as their needs grow), and when the product has genuine "aha moment" value that free users can experience, creating authentic desire to upgrade for expanded capability—rather than a free tier so limited it fails to demonstrate genuine value, or so generous that few users ever feel genuine need to upgrade.

**Tiered Pricing:** Works well when you have genuinely distinct customer segments with meaningfully different needs and willingness to pay, allowing you to capture value across this diversity rather than settling for either underpricing your highest-value customers or overpricing your more price-sensitive segment.

**The critical failure mode across all models:** Choosing a pricing model based on what's currently fashionable or what competitors happen to use, rather than genuine analysis of your specific product's value delivery pattern, customer acquisition dynamics, and target customer psychology and budget authority.

## **Usage-Based vs Seat-Based Pricing: Aligning Price With Value**

**Seat-based pricing** (charging per user/license) works well when value scales relatively predictably with the number of people using the product, and when this metric is easy for customers to understand and predict for their own budgeting purposes.

**Usage-based pricing** (charging based on actual consumption—API calls, data processed, transactions completed) works well when value delivery genuinely correlates more with usage intensity than simple headcount, and can feel more genuinely fair to customers (paying for what you actually use, rather than a fixed cost regardless of actual utilization)—though it can also create customer anxiety about unpredictable, potentially spiraling costs if usage isn't carefully monitored.

**The strategic question underlying this choice:** What pricing metric genuinely aligns with the value your customers actually receive, in a way that feels fair and predictable to them while still allowing your business to capture appropriate value as customers derive more benefit from your product over time?

## **Pricing Experiments: Genuine Rigor Required**

Given pricing's outsized impact on both revenue and customer psychology/trust, pricing experiments deserve particular methodological rigor.

**Why pricing A/B tests are genuinely tricky:** Unlike typical feature A/B tests, pricing experiments can create genuine fairness concerns (different customers paying different prices for identical value can feel deeply unfair if discovered, potentially generating significant backlash beyond the immediate experiment's scope) and can have longer-term effects that simple short-term conversion metrics don't fully capture (a lower price might convert more customers immediately while training the market to expect and demand that lower price point long-term).

**Practical approaches to pricing experimentation:**

**Cohort-based testing** (different pricing for genuinely distinct customer segments or time periods) rather than randomly showing different prices to similar customers simultaneously, reducing the fairness-perception risk of customers discovering they're paying different prices for identical value.

**Willingness-to-pay research** (Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter and similar methodologies) that directly asks customers about their price perceptions across a range of price points, providing genuine signal about pricing psychology without the risks of live pricing experiments.

**Grandfather clauses for existing customers** when testing price increases, protecting existing customer relationships and trust while still gathering genuine signal about new customer price sensitivity at different price points.

## **The Danger of Underpricing: Leaving Money (And Signal) on the Table**

While overpricing risks are more intuitively obvious (customers won't buy, or will churn), underpricing carries genuine, often underappreciated risks worth explicit attention.

**Underpricing signals lower value/quality** than customers might otherwise perceive, particularly in categories where price serves as a genuine quality signal in the absence of other clear differentiation information (unfamiliar brands in categories like professional services, luxury goods, or complex B2B software where genuine quality is hard to assess before purchase).

**Underpricing constrains your ability to invest** in genuine product improvement, customer support, and sustainable business operations—a chronically underpriced business often struggles to invest adequately in the very product quality and service that would justify higher pricing, creating a genuine downward spiral.

**Underpricing makes future price increases more painful** (both practically and psychologically, given loss aversion discussed above) than if you'd priced more appropriately from the start, since customers anchor on your initial price as their expectation baseline.

---

## **The Danger of Pricing Too High Too Early**

Conversely, pricing too aggressively before you've genuinely validated product-market fit and value delivery creates its own distinct risks.

**Premature high pricing can mask genuine product-market fit problems**, since the revenue generated from a smaller number of high-paying customers might create an illusion of business health that masks the reality that your broader target market genuinely isn't finding sufficient value to justify that price point.

**High pricing before genuine value delivery is proven** can create disproportionately vocal, disappointed customers (who paid a premium price and feel correspondingly higher expectations that weren't met), generating negative word-of-mouth that's particularly damaging during early-stage market education and reputation-building phases.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Pricing decisions require genuine psychological sophistication beyond simple economic rationality—understanding anchoring effects, loss aversion, charm pricing, and choice paralysis, rather than treating pricing as a purely competitive benchmarking exercise.

Choose between freemium, premium, and tiered models based on genuine analysis of your specific value delivery pattern and target customer psychology, not current market fashion. Align your pricing metric (seat-based versus usage-based) with genuine value delivery patterns that feel fair and predictable to customers.

Approach pricing experiments with particular methodological rigor given the fairness concerns and longer-term market-training effects that distinguish pricing experiments from typical feature experiments—favor cohort-based testing, willingness-to-pay research, and grandfather clauses over live differential pricing to similar customers.

Understand both underpricing risks (quality signaling problems, constrained ability to invest in genuine improvement, painful future price increases) and premature-high-pricing risks (masked product-market fit problems, disproportionate customer disappointment)—neither extreme serves sustainable business health.
