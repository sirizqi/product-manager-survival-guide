## **The Company With Twelve Different PRD Templates**

When I joined a mid-sized company as a Director of Product, I asked to see examples of recent PRDs across different teams to understand current practice. What I received was genuinely startling: twelve different PM teams were using twelve meaningfully different PRD templates, formats, and even fundamental structures. Some included explicit success metrics; others didn't mention metrics at all. Some had detailed technical requirement sections; others left this entirely to informal conversation with engineering.

This wasn't really about template preference or stylistic variation—it created genuine, costly friction. Engineers working across multiple product teams had to mentally context-switch between completely different documentation conventions. New PM hires had no consistent onboarding reference for "how we actually do this here." Cross-team collaboration on shared initiatives required constant translation between different teams' incompatible planning and communication formats.

Nobody had maliciously created this fragmentation—it had simply emerged organically as each team, operating independently without any deliberate coordination mechanism, developed its own practices in isolation. This is precisely the kind of problem Product Operations exists to solve: **not glamorous, strategic product decision-making, but the essential, often invisible infrastructure work of ensuring product teams can operate efficiently and consistently at scale, without every team reinventing foundational processes from scratch.**

---

## **What Product Operations Actually Encompasses**

Product Operations (often called "ProdOps," echoing the earlier emergence of "RevOps" and "DevOps" as parallel operational disciplines) has emerged as organizations scale beyond what informal, ad-hoc coordination between individual PMs and teams can effectively support.

**Core areas of Product Operations responsibility:**

**Tooling and infrastructure standardization**—ensuring the organization uses consistent, well-integrated tools for roadmapping, user research repositories, analytics, and project tracking, rather than each team independently selecting and implementing their own preferred tools with limited interoperability.

**Process and template standardization**—developing (and periodically refining) consistent frameworks for PRDs, discovery processes, prioritization methodologies, and stakeholder communication, providing genuine consistency without necessarily eliminating all team-specific flexibility where genuinely warranted.

**Data and metrics infrastructure**—ensuring consistent definition and tracking of key metrics across product teams, so that "activation rate" or "retention" means the same specific thing across different teams' dashboards, rather than each team defining these terms slightly differently in ways that make cross-team comparison and organizational-level reporting genuinely difficult.

**Onboarding and training**—developing systematic onboarding resources for new PM hires, ensuring consistent baseline understanding of organizational practices, tools, and expectations, rather than each new PM having to organically discover "how things work here" through scattered, inconsistent informal mentorship.

**Cross-functional coordination support**—facilitating the kind of cross-team syncs, shared resource coordination, and dependency management discussed in Chapter 24's growth-stage scaling challenges, providing dedicated organizational support for this coordination rather than leaving it purely to informal, ad-hoc effort from individual PMs already stretched thin with their own product responsibilities.

## **When Does Product Operations Become Necessary?**

Product Operations as a dedicated function or role typically becomes genuinely valuable once an organization reaches sufficient scale that informal coordination between individual PMs and teams starts breaking down—commonly somewhere in the growth-stage territory discussed in Chapter 24, though the specific threshold varies by organization.

**Signs you likely need more deliberate Product Operations investment:**

- Multiple PM teams have developed meaningfully inconsistent practices (documentation formats, prioritization approaches, metrics definitions) that create genuine friction for cross-team collaboration or new hire onboarding
- Significant time is spent in each planning cycle re-solving coordination problems that a standardized process could handle more efficiently
- New PM hires report significant confusion or inconsistency in understanding "how we actually do things here" across different parts of the organization
- Leadership struggles to get consistent, comparable reporting across different product areas due to inconsistent metrics definitions or reporting formats

**A caution against premature Product Operations investment:** Just as we discussed in Chapter 23's warning against importing big-company process into small startups, dedicating significant resources to formal Product Operations infrastructure before genuine organizational scale warrants it can create unnecessary overhead and rigidity for teams that would still benefit from the flexibility and speed of more informal, ad-hoc coordination.

## **Building Effective Documentation Systems**

Beyond simply standardizing on a single template, effective product documentation systems require genuine thought about discoverability and maintenance.

**The "documentation graveyard" problem:** Many organizations create seemingly thorough documentation systems (detailed wikis, comprehensive Notion databases) that gradually become unreliable and unused because they're not actively maintained—outdated information persists alongside current information without clear signaling about what's still accurate, eventually training team members to distrust and bypass the documentation system entirely in favor of just asking colleagues directly.

**Practical approaches to sustainable documentation:**

**Assign genuine ownership for keeping key documentation current**, rather than treating documentation as a one-time creation effort with no ongoing maintenance responsibility.

**Build lightweight review/expiration mechanisms** that flag documentation for review after a certain period, rather than allowing information to persist indefinitely without any prompt to verify continued accuracy.

**Prioritize documenting the specific, high-value information that's genuinely hard to reconstruct** (the reasoning behind significant past decisions, hard-won lessons from specific past initiatives) over exhaustively documenting every minor process detail that's relatively easy to simply ask about when needed.

## **Operational Excellence Without Bureaucratic Overreach**

The central tension Product Operations must navigate: providing genuine consistency and efficiency benefits without calcifying into the kind of excessive, rigid bureaucracy discussed critically in Chapter 25's enterprise bureaucracy exploration.

**Practical principles for avoiding this overreach:**

**Standardize the genuinely valuable consistency points** (core metrics definitions, essential documentation elements that enable cross-team understanding) while preserving flexibility for team-specific adaptation where genuine differences in product type, team size, or working style warrant it—not every process detail needs uniform standardization across genuinely different contexts.

**Involve genuine practitioner input in process design**, rather than Product Operations functioning as a purely top-down function dictating processes to PM teams without their meaningful input—processes designed with genuine practitioner collaboration tend to actually get adopted and followed, versus imposed processes that teams quietly route around or comply with only superficially.

**Regularly revisit and prune processes that have outlived their usefulness**, rather than allowing process accumulation to continue indefinitely without corresponding removal of processes that no longer serve genuine current needs—organizational process, like technical debt, can accumulate unless deliberately, periodically reviewed and pruned.

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Product Operations addresses genuine, costly friction that emerges as organizations scale beyond what informal, ad-hoc coordination between individual PMs and teams can effectively support—standardizing tooling, documentation templates, metrics definitions, and onboarding processes without necessarily eliminating all team-specific flexibility.

This investment becomes genuinely valuable once organizations reach sufficient scale that inconsistent practices create real friction, though premature investment before genuine scale warrants it risks unnecessary overhead and rigidity for teams that would still benefit from informal coordination's speed and flexibility.

Build sustainable documentation systems through genuine ownership assignment and review mechanisms, avoiding the "documentation graveyard" problem where outdated information erodes trust in documentation systems generally. Navigate the tension between valuable consistency and bureaucratic overreach by standardizing genuinely high-value consistency points while preserving warranted flexibility, involving genuine practitioner input in process design, and regularly pruning processes that have outlived their usefulness.
