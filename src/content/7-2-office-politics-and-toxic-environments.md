## **The Meeting Where I Realized I Was Being Set Up**

I once presented a product proposal that a colleague had privately encouraged me to develop, offering what seemed like genuine, supportive feedback during our one-on-one preparation conversations. In the actual leadership review meeting, that same colleague publicly criticized the proposal using nearly identical language to concerns I'd already addressed based on his own earlier private feedback—creating the appearance that I'd ignored his input, when in reality I'd incorporated it and he was now taking credit for "catching" issues in a version that no longer actually contained them.

It took me embarrassingly long to recognize what had happened: this wasn't an oversight or miscommunication. It was a deliberate pattern, and once I started paying attention, I noticed it had happened before, in slightly different forms, with the same colleague. Private encouragement, followed by public undermining, consistently positioning himself as more insightful than his colleagues in front of leadership who mattered for promotion and resource decisions.

This chapter exists because Chapter 12 (Stakeholder Management) addressed _healthy_ organizational politics—navigating legitimate differences in perspective and interest among reasonable people. This chapter addresses something genuinely different: **recognizing when you've moved beyond ordinary workplace friction into a genuinely toxic dynamic that healthy stakeholder management strategies cannot adequately address, and understanding what your actual options are once you've made that determination.**

## **Distinguishing Healthy Friction From Genuine Toxicity**

This distinction matters enormously, because misdiagnosing healthy friction as toxicity can lead to unnecessary conflict or premature departure from otherwise good opportunities, while misdiagnosing genuine toxicity as "normal friction to push through" can lead to prolonged, genuinely harmful exposure to dysfunction that deserves more decisive response.

**Healthy friction typically looks like:**

- Legitimate disagreement about strategy, priorities, or approach, even when expressed with some genuine frustration or passion
- Direct, if sometimes uncomfortable, confrontation of disagreements openly, even in front of others
- Criticism focused on ideas, work, or decisions—not personal character attacks
- Willingness to genuinely reconsider positions when presented with compelling counter-evidence or reasoning
- Conflict that resolves, even if not always in your preferred direction, and doesn't create lingering, escalating resentment

**Toxic dynamics typically involve:**

- Consistent pattern of credit-stealing or blame-shifting, particularly when it serves someone's visible positioning to influential decision-makers
- Information deliberately withheld or distorted to gain advantage, rather than genuine miscommunication or oversight
- Personal attacks or undermining conducted privately/behind someone's back, rather than direct confrontation
- Retaliation (subtle or overt) against those who raise honest concerns or disagree with influential figures
- A pattern where the same specific behaviors recur despite direct feedback or attempted resolution, suggesting deliberate strategy rather than occasional lapse

---

## **Common Toxic Patterns Specific to Product Organizations**

**The Credit Vampire:** Consistently positions themselves as the source of good ideas or successful outcomes that were genuinely collaborative or, in some cases, primarily driven by someone else's work—often skilled at subtle framing in meetings and written communication that gradually shifts perceived ownership without any single, clearly identifiable instance of outright lying.

**The Strategic Saboteur:** Privately encourages a colleague's approach or agrees with a plan, then publicly criticizes or undermines it when doing so serves their own positioning—exactly the pattern from this chapter's opening story.

**The Information Hoarder:** Withholds genuinely relevant information or context specifically to maintain personal power/relevance, rather than genuine oversight or reasonable confidentiality constraints—creating artificial dependency on themselves and undermining others' ability to make well-informed decisions.

**The Blame Deflector:** Consistently, systematically avoids any personal accountability for mistakes or failures, instead constructing narratives (sometimes subtle, sometimes blatant) that shift responsibility to others, regardless of actual facts.

**The Favoritism Enabler:** Makes decisions (resourcing, opportunities, recognition) based primarily on personal relationships or loyalty rather than legitimate merit or business reasoning, creating genuine unfairness that erodes broader team trust and psychological safety.

## **Navigating Layoffs and Organizational Uncertainty**

Beyond individual toxic dynamics, broader organizational uncertainty (layoffs, restructuring, leadership changes) creates genuine, distinct challenges worth direct treatment.

**During periods of layoff uncertainty:**

**Resist the urge toward purely self-protective, zero-sum behavior** that can further poison team dynamics during an already stressful period—genuine collaborative behavior, even amid uncertainty, tends to serve both your own reputation and broader team wellbeing better than visible self-interested maneuvering.

**Seek genuine clarity where possible, while accepting genuine uncertainty where clarity isn't available.** It's reasonable to ask direct, appropriate questions of your management about your role's stability and the broader organizational situation, while also recognizing that leadership may genuinely not have complete information to share, or may have legitimate constraints on what they can communicate during sensitive periods.

**Maintain genuine documentation of your contributions and impact** during uncertain periods—not from a place of paranoid self-protection, but as reasonable professional practice that serves you well whether the organization stabilizes or restructuring affects your specific role.

**If you are affected by layoffs**, recognize that this is frequently a reflection of broader business/financial conditions rather than a genuine reflection of your individual value or capability—this is genuinely important to internalize, even though it's admittedly difficult advice to fully absorb emotionally when actually experiencing this specific situation.

## **When and How to Address Toxic Behavior Directly**

Before considering more dramatic responses (escalation, departure), direct address is often worth attempting, when it can be done safely and without excessive personal risk.

**Practical approaches:**

**Address specific behaviors with specific examples**, rather than vague, general complaints that are easy to dismiss or deny. "In yesterday's meeting, you raised concerns using language nearly identical to feedback you gave me privately last week, in a way that made it appear I hadn't incorporated your input" is more addressable than "I feel like you're undermining me."

**Give genuine benefit of the doubt initially**, since not every instance of frustrating behavior reflects deliberate toxicity—sometimes what feels like intentional undermining reflects genuine miscommunication, differing memory of prior conversations, or unintentional impact rather than deliberate malice.

**Document patterns over time**, particularly if initial direct address doesn't resolve the behavior, since a documented pattern of specific instances provides more credible, actionable information than a single, isolated complaint if escalation eventually becomes necessary.

---

## **Escalation: When and How**

If direct address doesn't resolve genuinely toxic patterns, escalation to management, HR, or other appropriate organizational channels may become necessary.

**Considerations for effective escalation:**

**Focus on specific, observable behaviors and their business impact**, rather than purely personal characterizations—"this pattern has resulted in [specific negative outcome]" is generally more actionable for organizational response than "this person is toxic," even when your underlying assessment might genuinely be the latter.

**Understand your organization's actual track record on handling these situations** before escalating, if possible—some organizations genuinely take these concerns seriously and act appropriately; others have a track record of inadequate response or even retaliation against those who raise concerns, which should inform your approach and expectations.

**Recognize that escalation carries genuine risk**, even in well-intentioned organizations—the person you're raising concerns about may have more organizational power or credibility than you currently do, and even legitimate concerns can sometimes result in you being perceived as "difficult" rather than the underlying toxic behavior being adequately addressed.

---

## **Knowing When to Leave**

Sometimes, despite genuine effort at direct address and appropriate escalation, toxic dynamics persist, or the broader organizational culture proves genuinely unable or unwilling to address serious dysfunction. In these situations, departure becomes a legitimate, sometimes necessary consideration.

**Signals suggesting departure deserves serious consideration:**

- Toxic patterns persist despite genuine, appropriate attempts at direct address and/or escalation
- The toxic behavior is coming from someone with significant organizational power/protection, making meaningful resolution genuinely unlikely regardless of your efforts
- The broader organizational culture shows a consistent pattern of tolerating or even implicitly rewarding toxic behavior, suggesting this isn't an isolated individual problem but a systemic cultural issue
- Your own wellbeing (connecting back to Chapter 35's burnout discussion) is genuinely, significantly suffering as a result of prolonged exposure to this dynamic

**This is genuinely difficult, consequential advice to act on**, particularly given genuine financial and career considerations that complicate simple "just leave" advice. But it's worth stating directly: **remaining indefinitely in a genuinely toxic environment, hoping it will eventually resolve through your own continued effort alone, often extracts a genuine, significant cost to your wellbeing and career development that deserves honest, clear-eyed consideration against the genuine risks and challenges of departure.**

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Distinguish healthy organizational friction (legitimate disagreement, direct confrontation, idea-focused criticism, genuine conflict resolution) from genuine toxicity (credit-stealing, information manipulation, personal undermining, retaliation, persistent patterns despite feedback)—misdiagnosing either direction creates genuine costs.

Recognize common toxic patterns specific to product organizations (Credit Vampires, Strategic Saboteurs, Information Hoarders, Blame Deflectors, Favoritism Enablers), and navigate organizational uncertainty (layoffs, restructuring) by resisting zero-sum self-protective behavior while maintaining reasonable documentation and genuine professional practice.

Address toxic behavior directly when safely possible, using specific examples rather than vague complaints, before considering escalation—understanding that escalation carries genuine risk and effectiveness varies significantly by organizational culture and track record.

When genuine, persistent toxicity resists direct address and appropriate escalation, or when organizational culture broadly tolerates such dysfunction, departure becomes a legitimate, sometimes necessary consideration—remaining indefinitely in genuinely toxic conditions extracts real costs to wellbeing and career development that deserve honest, clear-eyed weighing against departure's genuine challenges.
