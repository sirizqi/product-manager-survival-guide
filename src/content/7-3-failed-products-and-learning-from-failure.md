## **The Post-Mortem That Changed Nothing**

I once sat through a post-mortem for a failed product launch that, on paper, looked like a genuine learning exercise. We spent two hours documenting what went wrong: unclear requirements, insufficient user testing, unrealistic timeline pressure, poor cross-team communication. The resulting document was thorough, honest, and genuinely well-organized. Everyone nodded along, feeling appropriately reflective.

Six months later, a different team launched a different feature that failed for nearly identical reasons: unclear requirements, insufficient user testing, unrealistic timeline pressure, poor cross-team communication. When I mentioned the eerie similarity to the previous post-mortem, someone said, "Oh, was there a post-mortem for that? I don't think I ever saw it."

The document existed. It was honest and thorough. It changed absolutely nothing about actual future behavior, because it lived in a folder nobody revisited, contained no specific accountability for implementing its lessons, and was treated as a one-time cathartic ritual rather than genuine organizational learning infrastructure.

This chapter addresses both the mechanics of genuinely useful failure analysis, and the more personal, psychological dimension of processing failure as an individual—two related but distinct challenges that most professional resources address poorly or not at all.

## **Case Studies: Famous Product Failures and Their Genuine Lessons**

Before diving into process, let's ground this in genuine, well-documented examples that illustrate recurring failure patterns.

**Google Glass:** Ambitious, genuinely innovative technology that struggled with unclear use case definition, premature market introduction before addressing genuine social acceptance and privacy concerns, and a mismatch between an early-adopter-focused product and mainstream market readiness. The core lesson: technical innovation alone doesn't guarantee product-market fit, particularly when a product requires genuine behavioral and social norm shifts beyond simple feature adoption.

**Quibi:** A well-funded, high-profile short-form video platform that shut down within six months of launch, despite substantial celebrity content investment and genuine production quality. Widely analyzed failure factors included launching during COVID-19 (when the specific "watch on your commute" use case largely disappeared), a fundamental misjudgment of user behavior (people wanted to share and rewatch content across devices, which Quibi's mobile-only, non-shareable format explicitly prevented), and prioritizing content production investment over genuine platform/distribution strategy validation.

**Juicero:** A genuinely well-engineered, expensive juicing machine that became a symbol of Silicon Valley excess when it was discovered that the machine's proprietary juice packets could be squeezed by hand, without the machine at all, achieving essentially the same result. The lesson: sophisticated engineering solving a problem that didn't actually require that level of sophistication represents a fundamental product-market fit failure, regardless of genuine technical achievement.

**What these cases share, despite their differences:** In each case, genuine technical capability or production quality existed, but fundamental assumptions about user behavior, market timing, or genuine value proposition weren't adequately validated before significant resource investment—reinforcing this book's repeated emphasis on genuine discovery (Chapter 4) and honest product-market fit assessment (Chapter 10) over pure execution excellence.

## **Conducting Genuinely Effective Post-Mortems**

**The core problem with most post-mortems**, as illustrated in this chapter's opening story, isn't inadequate analysis—it's inadequate follow-through and organizational learning infrastructure.

**Elements of genuinely effective post-mortem practice:**

**Focus on specific, actionable process lessons, not just comprehensive documentation of what happened.** Instead of "communication was poor," identify specifically: "engineering and design weren't included in initial requirement discussions, leading to late-stage technical constraints that weren't surfaced until implementation had already begun." Specificity enables actual behavior change; vague documentation doesn't.

**Distinguish between process failures and outcome variance**, echoing Chapter 20's decision-making framework. Did this fail because of genuinely poor process/reasoning, or because of legitimately unpredictable external factors despite sound process? Conflating these leads to either unwarranted process overhaul for genuinely unlucky outcomes, or missed genuine learning opportunities when process problems get excused as "bad luck."

**Assign specific, tracked follow-up actions with clear ownership**, not just documented lessons that exist only as a historical record. If a post-mortem concludes that discovery timelines need adjustment, this needs a specific, tracked action item with clear ownership, not just a sentence buried in a document that nobody revisits.

**Actually revisit previous post-mortems at the start of future retrospectives or planning cycles**, explicitly checking whether previously identified lessons are being genuinely applied, rather than allowing each post-mortem to exist as an isolated, one-time exercise disconnected from genuine organizational memory and behavior change.

**Create genuine psychological safety for honest contribution**, ensuring post-mortems surface real, sometimes uncomfortable truths (including leadership or cross-functional partner missteps, not just individual contributor mistakes) rather than becoming sanitized documents that avoid genuinely naming uncomfortable but important causal factors.

## **Dealing with Personal Failure: The Psychological Dimension**

Beyond organizational post-mortem process, individual PMs need genuine, healthy strategies for processing personal professional failure—a topic career development resources often address with either excessive positivity ("failure is just learning!") or insufficient genuine support.

**Acknowledging genuine difficulty without excessive self-flagellation:**

Professional failure, particularly on initiatives you've personally championed and invested significant effort into, genuinely hurts—dismissing this reality with forced positivity ("every failure is a gift!") can feel invalidating and doesn't reflect genuine emotional processing. At the same time, excessive self-criticism or rumination that extends well beyond genuine reflective learning into prolonged self-punishment doesn't serve your genuine growth or wellbeing either.

**A healthier middle path:**

**Allow genuine acknowledgment of disappointment or frustration**, without needing to immediately reframe everything as positive learning before you've genuinely processed the actual difficulty of the situation.

**Separate your identity and worth from specific outcomes**, echoing Chapter 35's burnout discussion—a failed initiative reflects specific circumstances, decisions, and often factors genuinely outside your control, not a comprehensive verdict on your fundamental competence or worth as a professional or person.

**Conduct genuine, honest personal reflection** (similar in spirit to organizational post-mortems, but personally focused): What specifically was within your control that you'd genuinely do differently? What was outside your control that you couldn't have reasonably anticipated or addressed? This distinction matters for genuine learning versus unproductive, undifferentiated self-blame.

**Seek genuine support from trusted colleagues or mentors** who can provide honest, constructive perspective—not just comforting reassurance, but genuine, thoughtful reflection that helps you extract real learning while also maintaining perspective on the failure's actual scope and meaning.

## **Recovery and Bounce Back: Practical Considerations**

**Rebuilding confidence after significant failure** often benefits from deliberately taking on a subsequent project or decision where you can apply specific lessons learned, creating genuine evidence (to yourself, and often to relevant stakeholders) that you've genuinely grown from the experience, rather than remaining stuck in either excessive caution or unprocessed anxiety about repeating past mistakes.

**Rebuilding stakeholder trust** (if the failure affected how others perceive your judgment) often requires genuine transparency about what you've learned and how you're applying it, rather than either excessive apology that undermines confidence, or defensive minimization that fails to acknowledge legitimate concerns stakeholders might reasonably have developed.

**Avoiding the overcorrection trap**—swinging from genuine confidence to excessive risk-aversion following a significant failure, avoiding any future ambitious bets specifically to avoid the possibility of repeated failure. This overcorrection, while psychologically understandable, often serves your career and organization poorly compared to genuine, calibrated risk-taking informed by real lessons rather than blanket risk-avoidance driven by unprocessed fear.

## **The Difference Between Not Learning and Repeating Mistakes**

Sometimes the same type of mistake recurs not because genuine learning didn't occur, but because of a more subtle failure: **learning the wrong lesson, or a lesson too narrow to actually prevent recurrence in a somewhat different but structurally similar situation.**

**A practical check:** When conducting genuine reflection (whether organizational post-mortem or personal processing), explicitly ask: "Is the lesson I'm drawing narrow enough that it would only prevent this exact specific mistake, or broad enough to genuinely address the underlying pattern that could manifest in somewhat different specific circumstances?" Overly narrow lessons ("we should have tested this specific button placement more") often fail to prevent structurally similar future mistakes ("we should validate assumptions about user behavior before committing significant engineering resources," a broader lesson that would have addressed the specific button issue AND numerous other potential future scenarios sharing the same underlying pattern).

## **Chapter Summary: The TL;DR**

Genuinely famous product failures (Google Glass, Quibi, Juicero) consistently reveal that technical capability or production quality alone doesn't guarantee success—fundamental assumptions about user behavior, market timing, and genuine value proposition require the same discovery rigor and honest PMF assessment emphasized throughout this book.

Effective post-mortems require specific, actionable lessons (not vague documentation), clear distinction between process failures and legitimate outcome variance, assigned accountability for follow-up actions, and genuine, ongoing revisiting of previous lessons—addressing the common failure mode where thorough documentation exists but produces no actual behavior change.

Process personal professional failure through a healthy middle path: genuine acknowledgment of difficulty without excessive self-flagellation, honest separation of identity from specific outcomes, and genuine reflection distinguishing controllable factors from legitimate external circumstances.

Avoid both insufficient learning (repeating identical mistakes) and the subtler failure of learning overly narrow lessons that don't address underlying, broader patterns capable of manifesting in structurally similar but superficially different future situations.
